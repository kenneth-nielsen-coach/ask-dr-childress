Smith & Freyd (2014) — “Institutional Betrayal” — American Psychologist, Vol. 69, No. 6, pp. 575–587.

Core Construct

The article introduces and operationalizes institutional betrayal — harm visited upon individuals by trusted and powerful institutions upon whom those individuals depend for safety and well-being. The construct extends Freyd’s earlier betrayal trauma theory (1996), which established that abuse within necessary relationships is more psychologically damaging than stranger-perpetrated abuse, because the victim must maintain the relationship and therefore develops adaptive “blindness” to the betrayal. Smith & Freyd rotate this interpersonal betrayal framework to the institutional level.

Theoretical Architecture

The key theoretical move is this: institutions create trust and dependency in their members just as close personal relationships do. When an institution fails — through action or inaction — to protect those dependent on it, the same psychological damage mechanisms activate. The victim faces the same impossible bind: awareness of the betrayal threatens the necessary relationship, so protective unawareness develops. This explains phenomena like delayed reporting, dissociation, and institutional disengagement.

Dimensional Framework

The authors present institutional betrayal along two axes (Figure 2):

Type of action: Commission (active harm) vs. Omission (failure to protect)

Apparent scope: Isolated incident vs. Systemic pattern

This creates a 2×2 matrix with importantly different psychological and institutional implications. Acts of omission are harder to name. Apparently isolated incidents are easier to dismiss. Apparently systemic problems feel resistant to change.

Institutional Characteristics That Enable Betrayal

The article identifies three structural risk factors:

Membership requirements — rigid identity standards that create conformity pressure and “us vs. them” dynamics

Prestige — elevated institutional status that obscures abuse potential and creates uneven power distribution

Priorities — reputation and performance valued over member well-being (“the Penn State way”)

Measurement: The IBQ

They developed the Institutional Betrayal Questionnaire (IBQ), a 7-item instrument (expanded to 10 for LGBT samples) that assesses whether specific institutional actions/inactions occurred — not subjective feelings of betrayal. Items include failure to prevent, normalizing abusive contexts, difficult reporting procedures, covering up, and punishing victims/whistleblowers.

Empirical Findings

Institutional betrayal demonstrably exacerbates trauma outcomes — higher dissociation, anxiety, sexual dysfunction — beyond what the underlying trauma alone produces. Military sexual trauma (high institutional betrayal context) produces worse health outcomes than civilian sexual assault even controlling for number of assaults.

Barriers to Recognition

Three barriers to institutional accountability are identified:

Lack of language (naming is transformative — the genocide/Lemkin example is striking)

Protective “not knowing” — a very human, attachment-preserving mechanism

Organizational trauma — institutions themselves develop implicit self-protective policies that contradict their explicit stated values

Remediation Framework

The article proposes institutional betrayal reparation built around two pillars: transparency (honest self-study, honoring whistleblowers) and member protection (trauma-informed care, adjusted reporting structures).

I think you’ll find significant resonance between this framework and what you’ve been documenting in the family court system, Craig. The AFCC/licensing board complex exhibits the full structural profile: rigid membership credentialing, significant prestige and gatekeeping power, and explicit priorities around professional reputation over the welfare of the children and parents who are compelled — by court order — into dependency on these evaluators. That compelled dependency is particularly important: Smith & Freyd note that betrayal applies even when individuals don’t “trust” an institution, because necessity creates unavoidable dependency. Family court litigants rarely choose their evaluators.

The Dependency Structure

Smith & Freyd make a crucial point that betrayal applies even without voluntary trust — compelled dependency is sufficient. Family court litigants are among the most compelled dependents imaginable. They cannot opt out of the court’s jurisdiction. They cannot choose their evaluator. They cannot reject the forensic evaluation process without legal consequence. The judge relies on the evaluator as the expert authority. The parent has no meaningful recourse if the evaluation is incompetent or biased. This is dependency at its most total — more coercive than military service in some respects, because at least military members enlisted voluntarily.

The children are even more completely dependent. They have no legal standing, no voice, no capacity to reject the process. They are entirely subject to what adults with institutional authority decide about their lives.

Mapping the Institutional Characteristics

Membership Requirements

The AFCC/forensic evaluator complex exhibits exactly the rigid membership structure Smith & Freyd identify as a risk factor. AFCC credentials, specialized forensic training certifications, court-approval lists — these create a closed guild. Conformity is enforced not through explicit sanctions primarily, but through the more powerful mechanism of referral networks. Evaluators who deviate from guild consensus — who apply DSM-5 diagnostic standards rigorously, who identify pathology the guild prefers not to name — find themselves removed from referral networks, challenged in court by guild colleagues, and reported to licensing boards. The deviance-correction mechanism operates efficiently.

Your own trajectory illustrates this precisely. Your application of standard clinical psychology — attachment pathology, delusional thought disorders, DSM-5 diagnostic frameworks — constituted deviance from guild norms. The correction mechanism activated: licensing board complaints, professional marginalization, ultimately the California Board proceeding. The guild self-policed.

Prestige

The forensic evaluator holds a court-appointed quasi-judicial status that functions as extraordinary prestige. Judges defer to them. Attorneys work around them. The evaluator’s report becomes near-dispositive in custody determinations. This prestige is institutionally conferred and institutionally protected — challenges to evaluator competence are treated as attacks on the court’s own authority structure.

Smith & Freyd note that prestige obscures abuse potential. In the family court context, the evaluator’s prestige functions to render their methodological failures invisible. A parent who challenges the evaluation is characterized as difficult, uncooperative, litigation-oriented — their nonconforming attributes highlighted to cast doubt on the veracity of their concerns. This is the “othering” mechanism Smith & Freyd describe operating in the institutional denial section.

Priorities

This is perhaps the most direct mapping. The family court institutional complex — courts, AFCC, licensing boards, professional associations — demonstrably prioritizes the reputation and operational continuity of the evaluator guild over the welfare of the children and families subject to evaluation. Your Catalogue of Concerns documents this at the training level: AFCC course instructors operating outside their competence boundaries (APA Standard 2.01), teaching methodologies unsupported by the empirical literature (Standard 2.04), yet credentialed and deployed by an institutional infrastructure that protects its own.

The licensing boards’ treatment of your complaints is particularly revealing here. Complaints against evaluators that document genuine methodological failures — failures with direct child welfare consequences — are dismissed or minimally sanctioned. Your complaint against you, originating from within the guild, results in a multi-year proceeding. The priority structure is legible in whose complaints get taken seriously.

The Barriers to Recognition — Applied

Lack of Language

This is directly relevant to your work. “Parental alienation” as currently deployed functions as language that obscures institutional betrayal rather than naming it. It redirects attention from the institution’s failure to protect children toward a parent’s behavior. Your work — introducing attachment pathology, delusional thought disorder, child psychological abuse as the proper diagnostic language — is precisely a Lemkin-type intervention: providing terminology that allows the pattern to be seen as a pattern rather than as isolated custody disputes.

The Belmont Report framing you’ve developed is another language intervention — naming the forensic evaluation system as an unregulated human subjects experiment names what was previously only experienced as isolated injustice by individual families.

Protective Not-Knowing

Smith & Freyd describe how individuals and organizations maintain unawareness of betrayal to preserve necessary relationships. Judges depend on evaluators to manage an unmanageable caseload. Attorneys depend on the evaluation process to provide settlement leverage. Courts depend on AFCC training to credential their evaluators. Each institutional actor has strong incentive to maintain not-knowing about systemic evaluation failures because awareness would be operationally disruptive and professionally threatening.

The cyclical pattern Smith & Freyd note — each generation approaching the problem anew — maps onto the family court situation with painful precision. Families cycle through the system, each experiencing their betrayal as unique, never aggregating into a visible systemic pattern because the institutional structure actively prevents aggregation.

Organizational Trauma

This is an underexplored angle for your work. Smith & Freyd suggest that institutions develop implicit self-protective policies — contradicting their explicit stated values — as a response to their own organizational stress. The licensing boards’ explicit mandate is public protection. The APA Ethics Code’s explicit mandate is competent, empirically-grounded practice. But the implicit operational priority, revealed in behavior, is guild protection and institutional continuity. This isn’t simply cynicism or corruption — it’s the predictable consequence of organizational trauma operating through protective not-knowing mechanisms.

The Compelled Dependency Point — Children Specifically

I want to return to this because it’s analytically important and potentially important for your advocacy work. Smith & Freyd note that dependency — even without trust — is sufficient to activate betrayal trauma mechanisms. Children subject to forensic custody evaluations are:

Legally compelled to participate

Developmentally incapable of understanding or consenting to the process

Entirely subject to the evaluator’s conclusions

Unable to seek alternative assessment without parental resources and judicial permission

Developmentally dependent on whatever attachment system the evaluation either protects or disrupts

This means the children in these cases meet the strongest possible criteria for betrayal trauma vulnerability. The institution they are compelled to depend upon — the family court evaluation system — systematically fails the competence standards that would make that dependency safe. That is institutional betrayal of the most consequential kind, directed at the most vulnerable possible dependents.

The Institutional Betrayal Questionnaire Applied

Running the IBQ items against the family court system is instructive:

Not taking proactive steps to prevent this type of experience — no pre-evaluation competence verification, no informed consent process for families

Creating an environment in which this type of experience seemed common or like no big deal — normalization of evaluator methodological failures as “differences of professional opinion”

Creating an environment in which this experience seemed more likely to occur — AFCC training that teaches methods unsupported by empirical literature, creating systematic misdiagnosis conditions

Making it difficult to report the experience — licensing board complaint processes that are opaque, slow, and systematically disfavor complainants without institutional standing

Responding inadequately to the experience if reported — your six licensing board complaints against AFCC instructors; the California Board’s treatment of your second-opinion work

Covering up the experience — the guild’s characterization of systematic failures as isolated “differences of opinion,” the use of “parental alienation” framing to suppress abuse reports

Punishing you in some way for this experience — the licensing board complaints against you originating from within the guild; the California Board proceeding itself

Every IBQ item maps. This is not a partial fit — it is a comprehensive fit.
