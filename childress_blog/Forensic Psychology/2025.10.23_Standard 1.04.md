1.04 Informal Resolution of Ethical Violations

When psychologists believe that there may have been an ethical violation by another psychologist, they attempt to resolve the issue by bringing it to the attention of that individual, if an informal resolution appears appropriate and the intervention does not violate any confidentiality rights that may be involved.

As you are hopefully aware, a severe attachment pathology (i.e., disorganized attachment) in the context of child custody conflict involves a Path A – Path B differential diagnosis for its causal explanation:

Path A – Child Abuse by Targeted Parent: the targeted parent is abusing the child in some way, thereby creating the child’s self-protective rejection of the targeted parent (a disorganized attachment toward an authentically abusive parent; i.e., the child has no organized strategy to bond to the parent),

Path B – Psychological Child Abuse by Allied Parent: the allied parent is psychologically abusing the child by creating a shared (induced) persecutory delusion and false (factitious) attachment pathology in the child for primary and secondary gain to the allied parent (DSM-5 300.19 Factitious Disorder Imposed on Another). The primary and secondary gain to the allied parent for inducing false pathology in the child are:

Primary Gain – Regulatory Object: the child is used as a “regulatory object” to meet the emotional and psychological needs of the parent.

Secondary Gain – Court Manipulation: manipulating court decisions regarding child custody in their favor because of the child’s induced attachment pathology toward the other parent.

Secondary Gain: Spousal Abuse: gratification from the psychological and emotional spousal abuse of the targeted parent using the child as the spousal abuse weapon.

Duty to Protect

When a child rejects a parent (i.e., a disorganized attachment – no organized strategy to bond to the parent), three abuse diagnoses are of immediate consideration, 1) possible child abuse by the targeted parent creating authentic disorganized attachment pathology in the child toward that parent, 2) possible child psychological abuse by the allied parent who is creating a (shared/induced) persecutory delusion and false (factitious) attachment pathology in the child (for primary and secondary gain), and 3) possible spousal psychological abuse of the targeted parent by the allied parent using the child (and the child’s induced symptoms) as the spousal abuse weapon.

Given that three possible abuse diagnoses are within the scope of diagnostic consideration based on the nature and severity of the child’s displayed attachment pathology (i.e., a child rejecting a parent; a directional change in a primary motivational system) within the context of a court-involved child custody conflict, a duty to protect is active for all involved mental health professionals relative to one parent or the other, requiring them to conduct a proper risk assessment for the abuse concerns involved. When there are credible indicators of psychological control or induced beliefs causing severe disruption to parent-child attachment, Standard 3.04 requires considering duty-to-protect obligations and actions.

Standard 2.04 Bases for Scientific and Professional Judgments

Professional concerns surrounding Standard 2.01 become extended to concerns for Standard 2.04, i.e., that if you do not know the necessary established professional knowledge (e.g., attachment, delusional thought disorders, factitious disorders, child abuse and complex trauma, family systems) then you cannot apply the established scientific and professional knowledge of the discipline as the bases for your professional judgments.

2.04 Bases for Scientific and Professional Judgments 

Psychologists’ work is based upon established scientific and professional knowledge of the discipline.

Standard 9.01 Bases for Assessment

Further concerns emerge that if you do not know (2.01) and do not apply (2.04) the established scientific and professional knowledge of the discipline as the bases for your professional judgments, then your recommendations, diagnoses, and evaluative statements will not be based on sufficient information (2.04) needed to substantiate your findings.

9.01 Bases for Assessments

(a) Psychologists base the opinions contained in their recommendations, reports, and diagnostic or evaluative statements, including forensic testimony, on information and techniques sufficient to substantiate their findings. (See also Standard 2.04, Bases for Scientific and Professional Judgments.)
