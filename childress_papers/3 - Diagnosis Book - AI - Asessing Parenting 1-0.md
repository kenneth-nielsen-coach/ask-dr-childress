# Diagnosing Child Abuse in the Family Courts

Diagnosis Series — Initial Chapter Draft

### Dangerous Pathology and the Duty to Protect

There are three broad categories of dangerous pathology in clinical psychology: suicide, homicide, and abuse. Abuse includes child abuse, spousal or partner abuse, and elder abuse. When a mental health professional encounters a dangerous pathology, a duty to protect becomes active.

That duty requires three actions. First, the mental health professional must conduct a proper risk assessment personally or ensure that a proper risk assessment is conducted for the danger involved. Second, the professional must take an affirmative protective action. Something must be done to increase safety. In a suicide case, for example, that might involve increasing the frequency of sessions or activating appropriate support networks with the necessary permissions. In a child abuse case, it may involve referral to Child Protective Services for investigation. Third, the professional must document both the findings of the risk assessment and the protective action taken in the patient's medical record. The practical rule is simple: if it is not documented in the chart, it did not happen.

These obligations are fundamental when evaluating court-involved family conflict because the child's symptoms may indicate an active abuse pathology. The task of the mental health professional is therefore not simply to describe family conflict. It is to identify the dangerous pathology accurately, conduct or ensure the proper risk assessment, take protective action, and document the findings.

### The Four Forms of Child Abuse

The DSM-5 identifies four forms of child abuse: physical abuse, sexual abuse, neglect, and psychological abuse. In the seminar, these diagnoses are discussed as V-code diagnoses that modify or accompany the primary psychiatric diagnosis and help identify the source and clinical context of the child's pathology. In the family-court pathology discussed in this Diagnosis Series, the primary diagnoses may include a persecutory delusional disorder in the allied parent, a shared or induced persecutory delusion in the child, and Factitious Disorder Imposed on Another. Child psychological abuse is then added because creating significant psychiatric pathology in a child, including a shared delusional disorder or a false factitious attachment pathology that destroys the child's attachment bond to the other parent, is child psychological abuse.

The reason to diagnose the full scope of the pathology is treatment planning. Every diagnosis identified in the assessment must be addressed in the treatment plan. If a clinically relevant pathology is not diagnosed, there is no mechanism for ensuring that it is treated.

### Diagnosis Guides Treatment

Each diagnosis identifies a different feature of the pathology and therefore a different treatment target.

For a shared delusional disorder, treatment addresses the delusional thought disorder. The seminar identifies protective separation from the primary case as a central intervention, followed by correction of the child's cognitive distortions and stabilization of normal-range perceptions. [APA source from seminar slide to verify and format.]

For Factitious Disorder Imposed on Another, treatment again involves protection of the child from the inducer and restoration of the child's normal-range functioning. In the family-court application described here, that includes restoration of the child's normal-range attachment bond to the targeted parent.

For child psychological abuse, treatment requires protection from the abusive parent, restoration of normal-range child functioning, and monitoring of the child's symptoms over time. I recommend daily ratings using a seven-point Likert scale for three domains: affection with the targeted parent, cooperation with the targeted parent, and social involvement. These ratings provide an outcome measure for tracking the child's recovery.

The protective component of treatment is therefore similar across these diagnoses, but the clinical targets are different. In the delusional thought disorder, treatment corrects cognitive distortions. In the factitious attachment pathology, treatment restores the normal-range attachment bond. In child psychological abuse, treatment monitors and reverses the child's abuse-related symptoms. This is why the complete diagnostic formulation matters.

### The Attachment System and the Initial Differential Diagnosis

Attachment is a motivational system. Its biological function is to motivate the child toward the parent for protection, survival, and care. Like other motivational systems, attachment has direction. When a child displays a strong directional reversal in attachment motivation and seeks to flee a parent, that symptom requires explanation.

The differential diagnosis is child abuse by one parent or the other.

The first rule-out is abuse by the parent the child is rejecting. In the general population, a child seeking to flee a parent raises concern that the parent may be abusive. In court-involved family conflict, however, a second pathway must also be assessed: the possibility that the allied parent is creating a false attachment pathology in the child through a shared persecutory delusion and Factitious Disorder Imposed on Another.

The child symptom therefore does not by itself identify which parent is responsible. It identifies the need for a proper child-abuse risk assessment. The central diagnostic question is: Which parent is creating the child's severe attachment pathology?

For the targeted parent, the differential includes physical abuse, sexual abuse, neglect, and psychological abuse. If those are ruled out, the assessment must then examine whether the allied

### Physical Abuse, Sexual Abuse, and Neglect: The Role of Child Protective

### Services

Concerns or suspicions involving physical abuse, sexual abuse, or neglect should be referred to Child Protective Services for investigation. The threshold for referral should be low because the first priorities are to protect the child and to protect the forensic evidence.

Mental health professionals and parents should not conduct their own investigative interviews of the child about possible physical abuse, sexual abuse, or neglect. Improper questioning can contaminate the child's report and damage the forensic evidence. The first report from the child is critically important, and Child Protective Services should conduct the formal investigative interview.

My clinical background includes direct work with Child Protective Services. I served as Clinical Director of a three-university assessment and treatment center for children ages zero to five in foster care, with Child Protective Services as the primary referral source. I have personally treated all four forms of child abuse and led multidisciplinary treatment teams that included CPS involvement. That experience shaped my approach to the respective roles of the treating clinician and the abuse investigator.

Even when I was directing a clinic serving abused children, if a new concern about sexual or physical abuse emerged during treatment, we did not probe the allegation ourselves. We made a new referral to Child Protective Services and allowed CPS to conduct the investigation. Once the investigation returned, treatment proceeded based on the findings.

In the framework presented in the seminar, substantiated and unsubstantiated CPS findings are treated as the operative findings for the involved mental health professionals. A substantiated finding means the abuse allegation was confirmed. An unsubstantiated finding means the abuse allegation was not confirmed. An inconclusive or uncertain finding remains an open clinical concern and continues as a rule-out diagnosis. New information arising in treatment may warrant a new CPS report and further investigation.

### Decisional Error in Child-Abuse Assessment

All diagnostic decisions carry the possibility of error. Child-abuse assessment can be understood using the standard concepts of true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives.

A true positive occurs when abuse is present and the assessment correctly identifies it. A true negative occurs when abuse is absent and the assessment correctly identifies its absence. A false positive occurs when abuse is absent but the assessment concludes that abuse occurred. A false negative occurs when abuse is present but the assessment concludes that it did not occur.

Where we set the decision threshold affects the balance among these outcomes. Increasing sensitivity to true abuse also increases the likelihood of false positives. Increasing the threshold

4

for finding abuse can increase false negatives. There is no purely mathematical answer to where the threshold should be set because the decision reflects the consequences attached to each type of error.

In child-abuse assessment, the false negative is particularly dangerous. A child who has disclosed genuine abuse and is not believed may be returned to the abusive parent. The child is then exposed to continuing danger and also learns that disclosure does not produce protection. That can be devastating. The child may never report the abuse again.

For that reason, my own clinical decision rule is strongly weighted toward avoiding false negatives when an older child makes a specific allegation of abuse. Children approximately nine years of age and older generally understand the meaning and consequences of an abuse allegation. When an older child makes a specific allegation, I give that allegation substantial weight because the cost of disbelieving a true allegation is so high.

If an older child is knowingly making a false allegation against a parent, that itself indicates serious dysfunction in the family and requires assessment. I am not willing to increase the risk of disbelieving a genuinely abused child simply to reduce the number of false-positive findings in children who are knowingly making false allegations.

The developmental picture is different for younger children. With children approximately six years of age and younger, truth, imagination, suggestion, and pretending are more developmentally fluid. Younger children are more susceptible to influence. Assessment therefore requires closer attention to the specificity of the report, the developmental capacities of the child, the context in which the allegation emerged, and possible prior questioning by an interested or motivated adult.

Again, however, investigative assessment of physical abuse, sexual abuse, and neglect should be conducted by Child Protective Services rather than by a treating mental health professional or parent.

### Psychological Child Abuse

Psychological child abuse requires a different assessment pathway. In the framework presented here, psychological abuse is not treated as a routine mandated CPS investigation because the assessment is substantially more complex and currently exceeds the ordinary training and investigative structure of Child Protective Services. Duty-to-protect obligations nevertheless remain active because psychological abuse is still dangerous pathology.

The assessment should therefore be conducted by a qualified mental health professional. If the involved professional is not competent to conduct the assessment, that professional must ensure that an appropriately qualified clinician conducts it.

My anchor definition of child psychological abuse is the creation of significant psychiatric pathology in the child. Creating a delusional thought disorder in the child is child psychological abuse. Creating a factitious disorder or factitious attachment pathology in the child is child psychological abuse. Creating psychiatric pathology that destroys the child's normal-range attachment bond to the other parent is child psychological abuse.

5

Other forms of psychological abuse may also be present. A parent may be excessively angry, hostile, critical, demeaning, or shaming toward the child. Those behaviors can represent psychologically abusive parenting. At the same time, assessment of these more broadly defined forms of psychological abuse must be sensitive to cultural, personal, and religious values. Psychologists should not impose one cultural model of parenting and pathologize parents simply because their values or parenting practices differ from the evaluator's own.

That is one reason I anchor the diagnosis in identifiable psychiatric pathology. When a parent is creating a delusional thought disorder or a factitious disorder in the child, the pathology is clinically clear and does not depend on the evaluator's personal preferences about parenting style.

### Normal-Range Parenting and the Limits of the Psychologist's Role

In the absence of child abuse, parents have the right to parent according to their cultural values, personal values, and religious values. Clinical psychology does not decide custody. The court decides custody.

The role of the psychologist is to identify pathology, diagnose it accurately, and provide treatment-related information. In the absence of child abuse, restricting a parent's time and involvement with the child can damage the child's attachment bond to that parent and thereby harm both the child and the parent. The psychologist's obligation is to avoid harm and provide accurate clinical information. The court then considers the full legal record and makes the custody decision.

This distinction is important. Doctors diagnose and treat pathology. Courts make legal decisions.

### The Family-Court Differential Diagnosis

When a child is seeking to flee a parent, the differential diagnosis contains two principal pathways.

The first is abuse by the targeted parent. The targeted parent may be physically abusing the child, sexually abusing the child, neglecting the child, or psychologically abusing the child. If abuse by the targeted parent is present, it must be identified, specified, placed on the treatment plan, and addressed.

The second pathway is abuse by the allied parent. If the targeted parent is not abusive, the assessment must determine whether the allied parent is psychologically abusing the child by creating a shared persecutory delusion and a false factitious attachment pathology for secondary gain to the allied parent.

There is also a second abuse victim who must be considered: the targeted parent. The allied parent may be using the child and the child's induced symptoms as a weapon of psychological spousal or partner abuse. That is also dangerous pathology and also activates duty-to-protect obligations.

The diagnostic assessment must therefore answer clear questions:

1. Is the targeted parent abusing the child? If yes, what type of abuse is present?

2. Is the allied parent psychologically abusing the child by creating a shared persecutory

delusion?

3. Is the allied parent psychologically abusing the child by creating a false factitious attachment

pathology for secondary gain to the parent?

4. Is the allied parent using the child and the child's induced pathology as a weapon of

psychological spousal or partner abuse against the targeted parent?

These questions require yes-or-no diagnostic answers, followed by specification of the pathology when it is present.

### A Six-Session Diagnostic Assessment Protocol

For assessment of this pathology, I generally recommend an approximately six-session protocol. The exact number can be expanded or reduced depending on the family, but six sessions provide a useful standard structure.

Session One: The Allied Parent I meet first with the allied parent to obtain the history and symptom information and, importantly, to hear the strongest allegations of abuse against the targeted parent. If the child is seeking to flee the targeted parent, I want to know exactly what the alleged abuse is.

Meeting with the allied parent first also allows me to conduct a mental status examination of thought and perception. I want to understand not only what the parent believes, but how the parent reaches those beliefs and processes contrary information.

Session Two: The Targeted Parent The second session is with the targeted parent. I obtain the history and symptom picture from that parent's perspective and assess the targeted parent's response to the allegations.

If substantial allegations were reported in the first session, I present those concerns to the targeted parent and evaluate the response. I am assessing whether the targeted parent is in fact abusive, how the parent accounts for the reported events, and whether the parent's behavior and psychological presentation are consistent with the allegations.

Session Three: The Child or Children The third session involves the primary affected child. In families with multiple children, the interview structure depends on their ages, symptom severity, and the possibility of sibling influence.

I generally want to interview the most affected child and often the older child first. Other children may need separate interviews to avoid influence from siblings who hold stronger views. Younger children may not require a formal interview depending on the circumstances, while adult children may sometimes provide useful historical information.

The purpose of the child interview is to understand why the child is seeking to flee the targeted parent. I obtain the child's account of the events and concerns and conduct an age-appropriate

7

examination of the child's reasoning and perceptions. Those reported incidents then become material for direct observation in the parent-child session.

Session Four: The Targeted Parent and Child Together The fourth session brings the targeted parent and child together so that the child's symptoms can be observed directly.

The question is no longer simply what each person reports. I want to see what the relationship actually looks like. Is the child frightened? Withdrawn? Hostile? Engaged? Does the child show severe anxiety in the parent's presence, or does the child's behavior look qualitatively different from the prior reports?

I use applied behavioral analysis and behavior-chain interviewing to examine the conflict incidents reported earlier. The parent and child walk through specific incidents step by step. This allows examination of antecedents, behavior, consequences, reinforcement patterns, and discrepancies in perception.

I also use response-to-intervention probes. For example: if the identified problems with the targeted parent were corrected, would the child then be willing to restore the relationship? A child who says, in effect, “Yes, if the problems were fixed, I would want the relationship,” is displaying a fundamentally different attachment response from a child who insists that the parent can never change and that no repair is possible under any circumstances.

Session Five: Feedback to the Allied Parent By the fifth session, I usually have a substantially clearer picture of the family pathology. I meet again with the allied parent, provide assessment feedback, and discuss treatment recommendations.

If the targeted parent has been found to be within normal-range parenting and not abusive, the allied parent's response becomes diagnostically informative. A normal-range parent who genuinely feared that the child was being abused should experience relief when a careful assessment finds that the child is safe.

When the allied parent is creating the pathology, a very different response may emerge. The parent may become obsessively fixated on preserving the protective-parent role and on empowering the child to continue rejecting the targeted parent. The parent may resist information that the targeted parent is not abusive and intensify efforts to maintain the child's belief that abuse is occurring.

In the trauma formulation described in this Diagnosis Series, this response reflects a reenactment narrative organized around three roles: abusive parent, victimized child, and protective parent. The allied parent occupies the protective-parent role and regulates unresolved trauma through the reenactment. Removing the premise that the child is being abused threatens that psychological organization. The parent's response to this intervention therefore provides additional diagnostic information.

8

Session Six: Feedback to the Targeted Parent The sixth session provides feedback to the targeted parent and discusses treatment recommendations.

If the allied parent is creating the pathology, the targeted parent will generally be receptive to a treatment plan directed toward restoring the child's normal-range functioning and repairing the parent-child relationship. If the targeted parent is the abusive parent, feedback identifying the abusive behavior may produce a very different psychological response and must be managed clinically and protectively.

The six-session structure is not mechanical. Its purpose is to obtain multiple perspectives, directly observe the parent-child relationship, test hypotheses against behavior, examine response to intervention, and answer the differential diagnosis.

### Child Abuse Risk Assessment Is Required by the Symptom

Severe attachment pathology in which a child is seeking to flee a parent requires a proper risk assessment for child abuse. The child's symptom itself creates the need for the differential diagnosis.

The assessment must determine whether the targeted parent is creating the child's attachment pathology through abuse or whether the allied parent is creating the pathology through psychological abuse, a shared persecutory delusion, and a false factitious attachment pathology.

This is the information the court needs. The court does not need psychologists to assume a quasi-judicial role and make custody decisions. It needs doctors to diagnose the pathology accurately, identify which parent is creating the danger, and provide treatment recommendations based on that diagnosis.

Clinical psychology should therefore return to its proper role: diagnosis and treatment of pathology. The court remains responsible for legal decisions about custody and parenting time.

### Spousal or Partner Psychological Abuse

The assessment must also include possible psychological abuse of the targeted parent. A child can become the instrument through which one parent psychologically abuses the other parent. In this pattern, the child's induced pathology and rejection of the targeted parent become the weapon of the spousal abuse.

The seminar identifies this as spouse or partner psychological abuse under the DSM-5 coding system. [DSM coding from seminar slide to verify and format.]

Duty-to-protect obligations apply to abuse generally. They do not end with the child. If there is reason to suspect that one parent is using the child and the child's induced psychiatric symptoms as a weapon of psychological abuse against the other parent, that pathology also requires assessment, diagnosis, and protective response.

### Disputed Diagnosis and Second Opinion

Any diagnosis submitted into an adversarial legal system will predictably be disputed. Healthcare already has a well-established response to disputed diagnosis: second opinion.

Second and even third opinions should therefore be routine in these cases, particularly because the consequences of diagnostic error are so serious. The court can designate a primary psychologist to conduct the diagnostic assessment and allow each litigant parent to retain a consulting psychologist who participates through telehealth.

The result is functionally similar to a three-doctor diagnostic panel. The primary psychologist conducts the assessment and writes the principal report. Each parent has access to an independent consultant. The consultants can observe, review the evidence, challenge errors, and contribute additional expertise.

This structure improves diagnostic accuracy and also improves treatment. The same consultants who participated in the initial assessment can remain available to the treating clinician as the case moves into intervention. Better assessment therefore produces better treatment planning and more effective ongoing consultation.

Diagnosis guides treatment. Treating abuse by the targeted parent and treating abuse by the allied parent require very different interventions. The diagnosis has to be accurate before the treatment plan can be accurate.

### Documenting the Symptoms

Disputed diagnoses also require clear documentation. Diagnosis is a pattern match between observed symptoms and diagnostic criteria. The clearer the symptoms are documented, the easier it becomes to evaluate whether the diagnosis is correct.

All involved mental health professionals should therefore document both the child's symptoms and the parenting practices of the targeted parent.

I developed two instruments for this purpose. The Diagnostic Checklist for Pathogenic Parenting documents professional judgments regarding the child's symptom pattern. The Parenting Practices Rating Scale documents professional judgments regarding whether the targeted parent's parenting is within normal range or within an abusive range.

When all involved professionals complete the same structured instruments, discrepancies become visible. Those discrepancies can then be examined directly rather than remaining hidden inside vague narrative reports.

### The Clinical Risk of Joining the Shared Delusion

One of the unusual dangers of a shared delusional disorder is that professionals can become incorporated into the pathology if they accept the delusional belief as true without conducting an adequate assessment.

If a mental health professional accepts a false persecutory belief and responds to the child as though the targeted parent is malevolently treating the child, that professional begins reinforcing

10

the child's false belief. If the pathology is child psychological abuse, the professional can thereby become a participant in the abuse process.

That risk extends beyond therapists. Courts, guardians ad litem, minor's counsel, evaluators, and other professionals can all become organized around the false belief if the underlying pathology is misdiagnosed.

This is why diagnostic accuracy is so important. A misdiagnosis of ADHD does not make the clinician part of the ADHD. A misdiagnosis of autism does not make the clinician part of the autism. But if a shared delusional disorder and induced child psychological abuse are mistaken for an accurate description of reality, the professional may begin reinforcing the very pathology that requires treatment.

The solution is not caution without diagnosis. The solution is competent assessment, clear documentation, appropriate risk assessment, consultation, and second opinion.

### Conclusion

Assessment of child abuse in court-involved family conflict begins with the child's symptom and proceeds through differential diagnosis.

When a child is seeking to flee a parent, the clinician must determine which parent is creating the pathology. The targeted parent may be abusing the child through physical abuse, sexual abuse, neglect, or psychological abuse. If those possibilities are ruled out, the clinician must assess whether the allied parent is creating a shared persecutory delusion, a factitious attachment pathology, and child psychological abuse. The assessment must also consider whether the child is being used as a weapon of psychological spousal or partner abuse against the targeted parent.

Dangerous pathology activates duty-to-protect obligations. The clinician must ensure a proper risk assessment, take affirmative protective action, and document the findings and response. Physical abuse, sexual abuse, and neglect require appropriate CPS investigation. Psychological child abuse requires competent clinical assessment. Disputed diagnoses benefit from second and third opinions, and the symptoms should be clearly documented so that the diagnosis can be evaluated rather than merely asserted.

The central task is straightforward: diagnose the pathology accurately, identify the source of danger, protect the child and other victims of abuse, and develop the treatment plan from the diagnosis.
