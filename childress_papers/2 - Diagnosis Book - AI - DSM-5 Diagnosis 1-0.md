# Diagnosing the DSM-5 Pathology in the Family Courts

### Diagnosis Guides Treatment

Throughout healthcare, diagnosis guides treatment. The treatment for cancer is different from the treatment for diabetes. We must first diagnose the pathology before we know how to treat it. Put more simply, we must first identify the problem before we know how to fix it. In this sense, diagnosis means identifying the pathology, pathology means the problem, and treatment means fixing the problem.

Families involved in the family courts have a problem. The task of professional psychology is to identify what is causing that problem so that an effective treatment plan can be developed.

### Established Knowledge and the DSM-5

APA Ethics Code Standard 2.04 requires psychologists to base their professional work on established scientific and professional knowledge of the discipline. The DSM-5, published by the American Psychiatric Association, represents an important body of established scientific and professional knowledge that should provide a foundation for diagnostic formulations and professional judgments.

The DSM-5 is not the only source of professional knowledge relevant to court-involved family conflict. Established knowledge from attachment theory, family systems, personality pathology, child abuse and complex trauma, and related domains should also inform diagnostic formulation and treatment planning. The DSM-5 provides a common diagnostic language and a baseline framework that allows professionals to communicate clearly about pathology such as schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, delusional disorders, factitious disorders, and other recognized conditions.

Mental health professionals may disagree with aspects of the DSM system or its categories, but the DSM nevertheless provides a professional baseline. It helps define recognized forms of psychopathology and gives clinicians a shared language for discussing the diagnosis. Additional knowledge from attachment, family systems, trauma, personality disorders, and other areas broadens and deepens the diagnostic understanding, but DSM-5 diagnosis should remain part of the foundation for diagnostic formulation.

### Differential Diagnosis

Across healthcare, diagnosis is typically approached through a differential diagnosis. The diagnostic process begins with the patient's symptoms and considers the range of possible explanations for those symptoms. As additional information is obtained, some diagnostic possibilities are ruled out and others become more strongly supported.

In court-involved attachment pathology, the symptom of central concern is severe attachment pathology in which a child is rejecting and seeking to flee a parent. Less severe forms of problematic parenting produce insecure attachment strategies that remain organized around attempts to bond to the parent. When a child is seeking to flee from a parent, the differential diagnosis concerns abusiverange parenting by one parent or the other.

Path A is abusive parenting by the targeted parent. The targeted parent may be physically, sexually, neglectfully, or psychologically abusing the child, thereby producing an authentic attachment pathology in the child's relationship with that parent.

Path B is psychological abuse by the allied parent. In this pathway, the allied parent creates a shared or induced persecutory delusion and a false or factitious attachment pathology in the child for secondary gain to the allied parent. The related DSM diagnoses include Delusional Disorder, Persecutory Type; Factitious Disorder Imposed on Another; Child Psychological Abuse; and, when the child and the child's induced symptoms are being used as a weapon against the targeted parent, Spouse or Partner Abuse, Psychological.

The diagnostic question is therefore not whether there is a problem. The child is displaying a severe attachment pathology. The diagnostic task is to identify which parent is producing the pathology and by what mechanism.

### Why a DSM-5 Diagnosis Matters

Every assessment should discuss the relevant DSM-5 diagnostic possibilities. If a report contains no diagnosis and no discussion of the relevant diagnostic differential, the report provides no professional explanation of what pathology is present or absent.

If child abuse is present, the court needs to know. If spousal abuse is present, the court needs to know. If a factitious disorder is being imposed on the child, the court needs to know. If a persecutory delusion is being induced in the child, the court needs to know. These diagnoses are directly relevant to treatment planning, child protection, and the court's decision-making.

A DSM-5 diagnosis therefore creates accountability in the assessment process. It makes clear which diagnostic possibilities were considered, which were ruled in, which were ruled out, and the basis for those decisions.

### Dangerous Pathology and Duty to Protect

Professional psychology recognizes three broad categories of dangerous pathology: suicide, homicide, and abuse. Abuse includes child abuse, spousal abuse, and elder abuse. When a mental health professional encounters dangerous pathology, duty-to-protect obligations become active.

The first obligation is to conduct a proper risk assessment for the danger involved, or to ensure that an appropriate risk assessment is conducted. In a suicide case, the clinician may conduct a suicide risk assessment directly or ensure that the person receives an appropriate emergency evaluation. In a child abuse case, the clinician may make the necessary referral to Child Protective Services or another appropriate child-protection authority.

The second obligation is to take affirmative protective action. The professional must do something to address the identified danger. Depending on the situation, that may involve increasing the frequency of sessions, activating available support systems, contacting appropriate authorities, facilitating access to a shelter or emergency resource, or taking other protective steps.

The third obligation is documentation. The risk assessment and the protective actions taken should be documented in the patient's medical record.

These obligations apply whenever dangerous pathology is reasonably at issue. In court-involved family conflict involving a child who is seeking to flee a parent, the differential diagnosis itself places possible child abuse and possible spousal abuse on the table. That activates the need for a proper risk assessment and a protective response.

### The Child-Abuse Differential

When a child is rejecting a parent, the differential diagnosis identifies two broad possibilities.

In Path A, the targeted parent is abusing the child and creating an authentic attachment pathology. If that is the source of the child's symptoms, the child abuse must be identified, diagnosed, and addressed through an appropriate treatment and protection plan.

In Path B, the targeted parent is not abusing the child. The source of the child's pathology is then the allied parent's psychological abuse of the child through the creation of a shared persecutory delusion and a false or factitious attachment pathology. This pathway also raises the possibility of psychological spousal abuse of the targeted parent through the use of the child and the child's induced symptoms as the weapon.

The differential diagnosis must therefore be conducted relative to each parent. The task is to determine whether the targeted parent is producing the pathology through abusive-range parenting or whether the allied parent is producing the pathology through psychological control, induced delusional belief, and factitious attachment pathology.

### Risk Assessment and Protective Action

In all court-involved custody conflict involving severe attachment pathology, a proper risk assessment should be conducted for the relevant differential diagnosis. The assessment needs to address the possibility of child abuse by the targeted parent, child psychological abuse by the allied parent, and psychological spousal abuse of the targeted parent when the child is being used as the instrument of that abuse.

The outcome of the assessment should be clearly documented. The court should be told which diagnoses were considered, which were supported, which were ruled out, and why. This is the information the court needs from professional psychology.

### Negligence and Failure to Assess Dangerous Pathology

A failure to conduct a proper risk assessment for dangerous pathology may constitute a negligent omission when there is a duty to act. In clinical practice, the standard question is what a reasonably prudent mental health professional would do under the same circumstances.

When suicide, homicide, or abuse is a reasonably considered possibility, the professionally appropriate response is to conduct or ensure a proper risk assessment, take affirmative protective action, and document what was done. Failure to take those steps raises serious professional concerns.

In the family courts, severe attachment pathology presents multiple possible abuse pathways. The child may be experiencing authentic abuse by the targeted parent, psychological abuse by the allied parent through induced delusional and factitious pathology, or both child and spousal abuse through the weaponization of the child. These are dangerous pathologies and require direct assessment.

### Professional Competence

The diagnostic demands of these cases require competence across several domains. Mental health professionals assessing severe attachment pathology need sufficient competence in attachment, child

abuse and complex trauma, delusional thought disorders, factitious disorders, personality pathology, and family systems.

If a professional is not competent to assess a relevant diagnostic possibility, consultation or referral is required. A lack of competence does not remove the diagnostic question. It means that the necessary expertise must be brought into the assessment.

The problem in the family courts is not solved by abandoning diagnosis. It is solved by improving diagnostic competence and returning to the established knowledge and methods of professional psychology.

### Conclusion

Diagnosis guides treatment. The family courts need clear clinical answers about the pathology affecting the child and family. When a child is rejecting and seeking to flee a parent, the mental health assessment should identify the relevant differential diagnosis, evaluate the possible abuse pathways, apply the DSM-5 and other established domains of professional knowledge, and return a clear diagnosis that can guide treatment and child-protection decisions.

The central question is straightforward: which parent is producing the child's severe attachment pathology, by what mechanism, and what diagnosis best describes that pathology? Once that is identified accurately, treatment and protection can follow.
