# **Forensic Custody Evaluations and the Return of Clinical Psychology** 

_Initial Rough Draft from Diagnosis Series Seminar Transcript_ 

C. A. Childress, Psy.D. 

## **Introduction** 

This chapter examines forensic custody evaluations in the family courts, the quasi-judicial role that forensic psychology developed in court-involved custody conflict, the historical circumstances that contributed to that development, and the need to return clinical psychology—diagnosis and treatment—to court-involved family conflict. 

From a clinical psychology perspective, high-conflict custody litigation is itself diagnostically meaningful. Normal-range parents possess empathy for the child and recognize the destructive impact that prolonged custody warfare can have on the child. They find ways to resolve disagreements and work together sufficiently to protect the child. When conflict becomes entrenched and litigation becomes chronic, at least one parent is likely presenting with high-conflict personality pathology involving an absence of empathy, commonly narcissistic, borderline, or dark-personality features. 

## **High-Conflict Custody Litigation and Personality Pathology** 

The narcissistic variant is vulnerable to rejection. Divorce creates a narcissistic injury, and narcissistic and dark personalities can become highly vengeful in response. To stabilize the injury, the parent may incorporate the child as a regulatory object: “I am not the rejected one; you are. The child is rejecting you.” In this way, the child becomes part of the parent’s psychological regulation of the divorce. 

The borderline variant is vulnerable to abandonment fears. Divorce activates those fears and can reactivate unresolved trauma. Free-floating fear and anxiety may then be displaced into the current family situation, producing heightened anxiety, repeated allegations, and a persistent perception of threat. 

The specific categorical diagnosis is less important here than the research-identified personality features. The relevant constellations include the dark triad, vulnerable dark triad, and dark tetrad. These constellations include narcissistic pathology, psychopathic features, Machiavellian manipulation, borderline features, and, in the dark tetrad, sadistic traits. 

Within this pathology, the narcissistic-borderline-dark personality parent can create false pathology in the child for secondary gain. The potential secondary gains include manipulating the court’s custody decisions, psychologically abusing the former spouse through the child, and using the child as a regulatory object to meet the parent’s own emotional and psychological needs.

## **Driving a Clinical Problem into the Legal System** 

A child rejecting a parent is a pathology. It belongs in the health-care system for diagnosis and treatment; it is not a crime. The pathological parent, however, can drive the child’s symptoms into the adversarial legal system and frame the problem as a custody dispute rather than a clinical disorder requiring diagnosis and treatment. 

The pathology is then used rather than treated. The parent does not want the symptoms to disappear because the symptoms serve a function: they support the parent’s position in litigation, enable continued conflict with the former spouse, and stabilize the parent’s own emotional structure following the rejection and abandonment inherent in divorce. The former spouse is effectively placed on trial for being a bad parent or a bad spouse. 

## **The Historical Origins of Forensic Custody Evaluations** 

The origins of forensic psychology in the family courts can be understood through the concept of staff splitting. As divorce became more common and high-conflict custody cases increased during the 1980s, professional psychology encountered more parents with narcissistic, borderline, and darkpersonality pathology. The splitting symptom in the family system—polarization into opposing sides with little or no middle ground—generated a parallel process among the professionals. 

Marsha Linehan describes staff splitting as a characteristic phenomenon in work with borderline personality disorder: professionals treating the same patient begin arguing and fighting about the patient, the treatment plan, or the behavior of other professionals. [Source quotation to verify and format from seminar PowerPoint.] 

That is what occurred in the family courts. Mental-health professionals became aligned with one side or the other, producing competing experts who mirrored the polarized family conflict. The problem professional psychology was trying to solve was therefore real: it needed a way to contain the parallel process of staff splitting. 

The solution that developed, however, was an experimental one. Forensic psychologists created a new quasi-judicial role for doctors in which the evaluator would investigate the family and make recommendations to the court about custody. With that role came a new assessment approach: the forensic custody evaluation. 

## **An Experimental Service-Delivery Model** 

A quasi-judicial role for doctors does not exist elsewhere in ordinary health care. Physicians and psychologists diagnose and treat pathology; judges make legal decisions. The forensic custody evaluation therefore represented a new experiment in service delivery to children and parents involved in family-court conflict. 

The first formal APA guidelines for child custody evaluations did not appear until 1994. Stahl and Simon describe the development of the field and note that forensic psychology, as a formal and organized field, remained in its adolescence and formative years. Their language remained essentially unchanged between the 2013 and 2020 editions of their work. [Source quotations and publication details to verify from seminar PowerPoint.]

By contrast, clinical psychology’s core health-care functions—diagnosis and treatment—are mature and established. Forensic custody evaluators moved away from the clinical mindset of diagnosis and treatment and developed a different forensic mindset, one oriented toward assisting the court rather than treating pathology. 

## **Guidelines Are Not a Scientific Foundation** 

APA custody-evaluation guidelines describe themselves as aspirational. They are intended to encourage systematic development and high-quality practice, but they are not mandatory, exhaustive, or applicable in every circumstance, and they do not supersede professional judgment. [APA guideline language to verify and format.] 

Guidelines can therefore provide useful practice suggestions, but they are not a substitute for a scientific or clinical foundation. The central concern is that parents and courts were not informed that this new quasi-judicial role and its associated assessment procedure were experimental rather than standard health-care practice. Nor were they offered the alternative of standard clinical care— diagnosis and treatment—as a meaningful option. 

## **The Forensic Mindset Versus the Clinical Mindset** 

Stahl and Simon explicitly contrast the forensic and clinical mindsets, observing that many custody evaluators trained in the clinical tradition bring a clinical mindset to forensic work even though clinical thinking is not considered appropriate to the forensic role. [Source quotation to verify and format.] 

That distinction is central. Clinical psychology asks: What pathology is present? What is the diagnosis? What treatment is indicated? The forensic role asks a different question: What information and recommendations should be provided to the court regarding custody? In my view, that shift moves the psychologist away from the proper role of a doctor. 

There is no quasi-judicial role for doctors. Doctors diagnose and treat pathology. Courts decide custody. 

## **Diagnostic Information and the AFCC Standards** 

The Association of Family and Conciliation Courts (AFCC) model standards illustrate the difference between the forensic and clinical approaches. Under its standards for presentation of findings and opinions, the AFCC cautions evaluators that diagnostic labels can divert attention from the focus of the evaluation and may be more prejudicial than probative. [AFCC quotation to verify and format.] 

A diagnosis, however, is not merely a label. Schizophrenia, autism, ADHD, delusional disorder, factitious disorder imposed on another, child psychological abuse, and spouse or partner psychological abuse are diagnoses. Diagnosis identifies the pathology that is present and guides treatment. 

If the family contains a persecutory delusion, a factitious disorder imposed on the child, child psychological abuse, or spouse or partner psychological abuse, those diagnoses are clinically relevant information. Describing these conditions as “deficiencies” rather than pathology obscures the nature and severity of the problem.

The court should receive the clinically relevant diagnostic information and then apply its own legal standards and decision-making criteria. Deciding whether a diagnosis is too prejudicial to one parent is not the doctor’s role. What is prejudicial to one litigant may be highly probative to the other, particularly when the diagnosis concerns child abuse or spousal abuse. 

## **The Problem of Withholding Diagnosis** 

When evaluators withhold clinically relevant diagnostic information, one litigant may be deprived of information necessary to present a child-protection or self-protection case. A targeted parent may need the court to understand that the child is sharing a persecutory delusion, that a false or factitious attachment pathology is being imposed on the child, that the child is being psychologically abused, or that the child is being used as a weapon of psychological spousal abuse. 

Those diagnoses are highly relevant to the court. Doctors should diagnose the pathology using established diagnostic systems, document the findings, and provide the information to the court. The court then decides what legal weight to give that information. 

## **Independent Criticism of Forensic Custody Evaluations** 

The New York Blue Ribbon Commission on Forensic Custody Evaluations identified serious problems with custody evaluations, including concerns that evaluators may rely on principles and methodologies of dubious validity and that defective reports can escape meaningful scrutiny because the parties may lack the resources to challenge them. The commission warned of potentially disastrous consequences for parents and children. [Commission quotations and vote details to verify from seminar PowerPoint.] 

A majority of commission members favored eliminating forensic custody evaluations entirely, citing concerns about bias, harm to children, limited scientific or legal value, and danger in cases involving domestic violence or child abuse. This independent review is consistent with the conclusion that forensic custody evaluations have failed as an experiment in service delivery. 

## **The Difficulty of Challenging a Custody Evaluation** 

Another structural problem is the difficulty of obtaining meaningful review. In health care, the appellate system for a disputed diagnosis is a second opinion. A forensic custody evaluation, by contrast, often takes six to nine months, can cost tens of thousands of dollars, and is difficult to replicate. Stahl and Simon acknowledge that evaluator recommendations have tremendous influence with the court and that overcoming those recommendations can be daunting, complex, and expensive. [Quotation to verify and format.] 

A clinical diagnostic assessment can be completed far more quickly and can include an active second opinion during the assessment itself. That is a familiar health-care process: diagnosis, consultation, second opinion, treatment planning.

## **The Non-Helper Role** 

Forensic psychology also describes the evaluator as occupying a non-helper role. Simon and Stahl explain that the evaluating forensic mental-health professional is not providing services whose goal is the alleviation of suffering or discomfort. [Quotation to verify and format.] 

That is fundamentally different from clinical practice. Doctors should help people. We diagnose pathology, protect patients from danger, and treat the identified disorder. The child and parents in court-involved conflict are suffering. A service-delivery model that explicitly does not seek to alleviate that suffering is the wrong model for health-care professionals. 

## **The Evaluator as an Agent of the State** 

The forensic evaluator is also described as an agent of the state when appointed by the court to conduct a custody evaluation and offer advisory recommendations regarding the child’s best interests. That confirms the quasi-judicial nature of the role. The evaluator is no longer functioning primarily as a doctor diagnosing and treating pathology; the evaluator is assisting the state in making a legal custody decision. 

Clinical psychology should return to the family courts. The court should make custody decisions. Mental-health professionals should diagnose and treat pathology and provide the court with accurate clinical information. 

## **Established Knowledge and the Return to Clinical Practice** 

APA Ethical Standard 2.04 requires psychologists to base their work on established scientific and professional knowledge of the discipline. The DSM diagnostic system is part of that established knowledge, as are the established domains of attachment, family systems, personality pathology, child abuse and complex trauma, and related clinical sciences. 

The proper clinical task is therefore to identify the pathology, conduct the appropriate differential diagnosis, and produce a treatment plan. In the family courts, the presence of a child rejecting a parent raises an immediate child-abuse differential. 

## **The Child-Abuse Differential Diagnosis** 

A child seeking to flee a parent or rejecting a parent reflects severe attachment pathology. The differential diagnosis is child abuse by one parent or the other. 

Path A is authentic attachment pathology caused by abusive-range parenting by the targeted parent. The possible diagnoses include child physical abuse, sexual abuse, neglect, or psychological abuse. 

Path B is false or factitious attachment pathology caused by the allied parent. This pathway includes a shared persecutory delusion, factitious attachment pathology imposed on the child for secondary gain to the allied parent, child psychological abuse, and psychological spouse or partner abuse of the targeted parent using the child and the child’s induced symptoms as a weapon. 

One way or the other, the symptom requires a child-abuse diagnosis and a proper risk assessment to determine which parent is producing the pathology.

## **Duty to Protect** 

There are three dangerous pathologies in clinical psychology: suicide, homicide, and abuse—child abuse, spouse or partner abuse, and elder abuse. When a mental-health professional encounters a dangerous pathology, duty-to-protect obligations become active. 

The professional must conduct, or ensure the conduct of, an appropriate risk assessment; take affirmative protective action; and document the assessment and protective action in the medical record or clinical documentation. In court-involved custody conflict involving severe attachment pathology, the relevant dangers include possible child abuse and possible psychological spousal abuse. 

A failure to conduct a proper risk assessment when the symptoms and context warrant one may represent a negligent omission. The relevant legal concept is negligence: failure to act with the level of care an ordinarily prudent professional would exercise under similar circumstances, including omissions where there is a duty to act. [Cornell Legal Information Institute quotation to verify and format.] 

## **Second and Third Opinions** 

Any diagnosis returned into an adversarial legal system will be disputed. Health care already has a solution for disputed diagnoses: second opinion. 

The court can appoint a primary psychologist to conduct a clinical diagnostic assessment. Each litigant can then appoint a second-opinion consultant to participate in that assessment, including through telehealth. The result is effectively a two- or three-doctor panel evaluating the same clinical information and diagnostic questions. 

This approach improves diagnostic accuracy and allows consultation to continue into treatment. The National Academies’ report Improving Diagnosis in Health Care emphasizes consultation and second opinion as ways to confirm or reject a working diagnosis and to obtain additional treatment information. [Quotation and citation to verify and format.] 

## **Why Diagnostic Accuracy Is Essential** 

When child abuse is a considered diagnosis, diagnostic accuracy is critical because the consequences of misdiagnosis are severe. With shared persecutory delusion, there is an additional risk: if a mental-health professional accepts the shared delusion as true, the professional can become incorporated into the pathology. 

If the pathology is psychological abuse of the child, the professional who validates and reinforces the false persecutory belief becomes part of the child-abuse process. If the child is being used as a weapon of psychological spousal abuse, the professional can also become part of that abuse. This is why the diagnosis must be made accurately and why second and third opinions are so important.
