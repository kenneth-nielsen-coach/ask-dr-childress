# Diagnosing Attachment Pathology in the Family

# Courts

Initial Rough Draft from Diagnosis Series Seminar Transcript

C. A. Childress, Psy.D.

A child rejecting a parent is an attachment pathology. It is a problem in the brain system for love and bonding. The clinical task is therefore to diagnose the attachment pathology accurately: identify the organization of the child's attachment system, identify the parenting conditions producing the symptoms, determine whether the attachment pathology is authentic or false, and establish the treatment implications that follow from the diagnosis.

This chapter begins with the attachment system itself because the diagnostic reasoning depends on understanding what attachment is, what it is designed to do, and how it becomes pathological. The attachment system is not simply a metaphor for relationships. It is a motivational system of the brain. Like other motivational systems—eating, pain, pleasure, sex—it has direction. A regulatory system fluctuates around a set point; a motivational system organizes behavior toward a goal. Attachment behavior is organized toward proximity and bonding with the parent.

Bowlby described the function of attachment behavior as protection from predators. [Verify quotation and page from seminar slide: Bowlby, Vol. 1, p. 227.] Across evolutionary history, a child who remained close to a protective parent was more likely to survive predation and starvation. A child motivated to separate from the parent would be at grave risk. The attachment system therefore developed as a powerful primary motivational system directing the child toward the parent. This becomes clinically important whenever a child is seeking to flee a parent, because such behavior represents a reversal in the ordinary direction of the attachment system.

### The Attachment System as a Goal-Directed Motivational System

The attachment system is described as goal-directed or goal-corrected. It seeks attachment bonding with the parent, and when a barrier interferes with that bonding the child changes strategy rather than abandoning the goal. The system remains organized toward the parent.

This is the foundation for understanding insecure attachment. Problematic parenting creates barriers to effective attachment bonding. In response, the child develops an alternative strategy for maintaining proximity or involvement with the parent. These strategies are not random. Attachment pathology expresses itself in characteristic ways.

There are three principal insecure or pathological attachment organizations relevant to this discussion. In an anxious-ambivalent attachment, the child displays high protest behavior to obtain the involvement of an inconsistently available parent. In an anxious-avoidant attachment, the child minimizes protest and becomes more self-reliant in order to avoid overwhelming a parent who is already withdrawn or overburdened. In a disorganized attachment, the child has no organized strategy for bonding to the parent. This last pattern is of particular concern because it is associated with abusive or psychotic-range parenting.

Protest behavior should be understood functionally. Anger, defiance, anxiety, sadness, clinging, and other high-intensity child behaviors can all serve the same attachment purpose: obtaining parental involvement. An inconsistently available parent may become more involved when the child protests strongly. Even negative involvement can be preferable, from the perspective of the attachment system, to being left psychologically or physically unprotected.

The avoidant strategy solves a different problem. When the parent is overwhelmed and tends to withdraw further in response to the child's needs, overt protest can make the situation worse. The child therefore reduces protest, becomes more self-reliant, and minimizes attachment demands in order to keep the parent as close and available as possible. The behavioral surface is different, but the motivational direction remains the same: the child is still trying to preserve the attachment bond.

The key diagnostic point is that both anxious-ambivalent and anxious-avoidant strategies remain strategies for bonding to the parent. The child may be distressed, angry, withdrawn, or self-reliant, but the attachment system remains oriented toward the parent. The child is trying to solve a barrier to bonding, not abandon the bond.

### Disorganized Attachment and a Directional Change in Attachment

### Motivation

Disorganized attachment is different. The child shows no organized strategy for bonding to the parent. In the family-court presentation of a child rejecting a parent, the child is not merely protesting or avoiding overstimulation; the child is seeking to flee the parent. That represents a directional change in a primary motivational system.

Instead of moving toward the parent and adjusting strategy when the relationship becomes difficult, the child is moving away. That is why this symptom is clinically significant. A child who has no organized strategy for bonding to a parent is displaying an attachment-system disturbance in the range associated with abusive or psychotic-range parenting. When this symptom is present, the assessment must determine which parent's behavior is producing it.

Attachment strategies are established early. By approximately five years of age, the child's characteristic attachment strategies are substantially stabilized. If a child has been secure, anxious-ambivalent, or anxious-avoidant with a parent through early and middle childhood and then abruptly develops a disorganized attachment organization later—seeking to flee a parent to whom the child had previously been bonded—the timing itself becomes clinically informative. The abrupt emergence marks the temporal onset of the conditions producing the disorganization.

This creates an immediate child-protection question. A later-emerging directional change in attachment motivation indicates that abusive or psychotic-range parenting has entered the system. The task is not to decide whether there is a problem. The symptom itself identifies a serious problem. The task is to identify the source.

### The Differential Diagnosis: Authentic or False Attachment Pathology

The central differential diagnosis is between an authentic attachment pathology caused by the parenting of the rejected or targeted parent and a false or factitious attachment pathology induced by the allied parent.

Path A is authentic attachment pathology. The targeted parent is engaging in abusive-range parenting, and the child's rejection is a self-protective response to that parent's behavior. The child's attachment system is responding to actual danger or severe relational disturbance in the targeted-parent relationship.

Path B is false attachment pathology. The targeted parent is functioning within the normal range and is available for attachment bonding, but the child nevertheless creates and maintains the barrier to the relationship. In this pathway, the child's behavior is being organized by psychological control and manipulation within the relationship with the allied parent. The child presents an inauthentic attachment pathology—a false or factitious attachment disorder imposed on the child for the needs and secondary gain of the allied parent.

The distinction is visible in the location of the barrier. In authentic attachment pathology, the barrier to bonding is located in the targeted parent's behavior. The parent is frightening, abusive, grossly insensitive, or otherwise creating the conditions that interfere with the child's ability to bond. In false attachment pathology, the targeted parent is available and seeking relationship, while the child rigidly maintains the barrier. The obstruction to bonding is being produced by the child under the influence of the other parent.

This is why the assessment cannot stop at the child's stated rejection. The clinician must identify the barrier, determine who is creating it, and evaluate whether the child's behavior is coherent with the actual parenting being received.

### Child Abuse as the Clinical Frame

When each parent is asserting that the other parent is causing the child's symptoms, child abuse is already part of the clinical differential. One parent alleges that the targeted parent's abusive behavior is causing the child's rejection. The targeted parent alleges that the allied parent's psychological control is creating a false attachment pathology in the child. Both accounts are allegations that a parent's behavior is producing serious pathology in the child.

The appropriate response is therefore a risk assessment for child abuse, not an open-ended custody evaluation and not years of therapy without diagnosis. Mental health professionals have duty-to-protect obligations when they encounter possible abuse. The court needs clinically grounded information identifying which parent is producing the disorganized attachment and what protective and treatment response follows.

For allegations of physical abuse, sexual abuse, or neglect, suspected abuse should be referred to Child Protective Services or the appropriate investigative agency. Mental health professionals should not contaminate forensic evidence through repeated or poorly structured questioning. Substantiated or unsubstantiated findings from a proper CPS investigation should be incorporated into the clinical assessment; unresolved or inconclusive concerns may remain open rule-outs and can be revisited if additional information emerges.

Psychological abuse requires a different clinical skill set. It is often more complex and relationally embedded, and it requires a competent psychological assessment of the family system, the child's symptoms, the parenting behavior, and the mental status of the involved parent.

### Psychological Control and the Induction of False Attachment Pathology

The process operating in Path B is psychological control, an established construct in professional psychology. Psychological control involves parental intrusion into the child's psychological world and attempts to manipulate the child's thoughts, feelings, identity, loyalties, and perceptions through mechanisms such as guilt induction, shame, anxiety, withdrawal of love, dominance, and relational pressure. [Verify Stone, Buehler, & Barber (2002) quotation from seminar slide.]

Psychological control is different from behavioral control. Behavioral control addresses what the child does. Psychological control reaches inside the child's subjective world and attempts to organize what the child thinks and feels. The parent may not directly instruct the child to reject the other parent. Instead, the parent shapes the child's emotional reality through indirect messages, guilt, anxiety, withdrawal, and relational consequences until the child's perceptions conform to the parent's needs.

This distinction matters because the pathology is not adequately described as simple 'badmouthing.' The psychologically controlling parent does not need to issue explicit instructions. The process is far more subtle and powerful. As described in the literature on emotional abuse, the intrusive parent attempts to manipulate the child's thoughts and feelings so that the child's psyche conforms to the parent's wishes. [Verify cited source and quotation from seminar slide.]

In the family-court presentation, psychological control can produce the child's rigid rejection of a normal-range parent. The allied parent's narcissistic-borderline personality features, splitting, anxiety, and need for regulatory control organize the child's perceptions. The child's attachment system is then recruited into the parent's unresolved trauma and relationship conflict.

### Assessment of Path A: Is the Targeted Parent Abusive?

The first assessment task is to rule in or rule out abusive-range parenting by the targeted parent. This is the most direct explanation for a child seeking to flee that parent, and it must be examined carefully.

The assessment should obtain specific behavioral incidents from both the child and the allied parent. Vague descriptions are not enough. The clinician needs to know what happened, when it happened, who did what, what each person was thinking and feeling, and what occurred before, during, and after the incident. These events should be examined using applied behavioral analysis and behavior-chain interviewing.

A behavioral analysis dissects the interaction step by step. What was the cue? What did the parent do? How did the child respond? How did that response then become the next cue for the parent? What consequences followed? The clinician is looking for abusive-range parenting, coercive cycles, threats, excessive anger, physical aggression, humiliation, frightening behavior, or other barriers to attachment bonding.

If abusive parenting is present, it should be diagnosed, documented, placed on the treatment plan, and addressed directly. If it is not present, the Path A explanation collapses and the assessment must turn fully toward Path B.

### Assessment of Path B: Persecutory Delusion, Splitting, and Personality

### Pathology

When abusive-range parenting by the targeted parent has been ruled out, the clinician must assess the allied parent for the psychotic-range or delusional process capable of producing the child's disorganized attachment.

The relevant assessment for a persecutory delusion is a mental status examination of thought and perception. This is an affirmative diagnosis based on observing the person's reasoning process in the clinical interview. The clinician evaluates whether the person's thinking remains linear and logical or whether it shifts, particularly around trauma-linked material, into emotionalassociational thinking.

The assessment follows the person's reasoning closely. When the clinician presents discrepant or contrary information and contains the discussion within a linear line of thought, the pressure on defensive processes increases. If a persecutory delusion is present, the fixed belief and the emotional-associational structure maintaining it become visible in the session. The diagnosis is not simply inferred from history; the clinician observes the thought disorder directly through the mental status examination.

Other characteristic features include splitting, cognitive rigidity, and absence of empathy. Splitting produces polarized perceptions of all-good and all-bad. Cognitive rigidity means that once the targeted parent has been defined as bad, dangerous, or inadequate, corrective information does not modify the conclusion. The conclusion remains fixed and new information is assimilated into the established belief.

The absence of empathy is particularly important in the child's presentation. The assessment is not simply whether the child can recite empathic language; it is whether the child displays a capacity for cruelty toward the targeted parent and justifies that cruelty as deserved. A useful probe may be to identify a rejecting statement as harsh or cruel and observe the child's response. In an induced narcissistic organization, the response may be that the parent deserves the rejection because the parent is bad or inadequate. The cruelty is morally justified through the same polarized value structure operating in the allied parent.

### Ambivalence as a Diagnostic Feature

Ambivalence helps distinguish authentic attachment injury from induced pathology. In authentic attachment disturbance, even when a parent is abusive, the child's attachment system continues to seek the parent. The child may be frightened, angry, avoidant, or conflicted, but the attachment motivation remains active. There is therefore ambivalence: part of the child wants protection from the parent and part of the child still wants the parent.

In the false attachment pathology, the child may display strikingly little ambivalence. The child does not simply report fear or hurt; the child can present a rigid moral judgment that the targeted

### Family Systems Features

The attachment pathology does not occur in isolation. The family system commonly displays a cross-generational coalition between the allied parent and the child against the targeted parent. The relationship can become enmeshed, and the family hierarchy can invert so that the child is positioned to judge, discipline, or reject the parent as though the child were in the parental role.

Statements such as 'I can't force the child' may function within this inverted hierarchy to surrender parental authority while simultaneously supporting the child's rejection. The child's apparent autonomy is therefore occurring inside a larger relational system in which the child's thoughts and feelings are being psychologically controlled and the child's rejection serves functions within the allied parent's unresolved trauma, personality organization, and conflict with the former spouse.

This is also where the trans-generational transmission of attachment trauma becomes clinically important. The allied parent's unresolved attachment trauma is not merely remembered; it is reenacted within the current family system. Narcissistic and borderline personality features organize the parent's perception of the divorce, the former spouse, and the child. The child becomes recruited into a trauma reenactment in which old relational templates are recreated with new participants. The child's attachment system becomes the vehicle through which the parent's unresolved trauma is transmitted into the next generation.

### Response to Intervention as a Diagnostic Probe

Response to intervention provides another way to test whether the attachment pathology is authentic or false. The treatment for an insecure attachment is to remove the barrier to bonding. If the barrier is located in the targeted parent's behavior and the parent is cooperative, correcting the problematic parenting should allow the child's attachment motivation to re-engage.

This can be made explicit in the treatment plan. The parent can be helped to become a more consistently warm, responsive, understanding, and developmentally appropriate parent. Discipline can be reduced and guidance-based strategies increased. Affection, attention, warmth, empathy, and relational repair can be strengthened. In practical terms, the treatment plan can include a 'wonderful mom' or 'wonderful dad' component: remove every plausible barrier to the child's attachment bonding and observe the child's response.

If the pathology is authentic, the child's attachment system should respond to the improved parenting. The child wants an available, loving parent, and once the barrier is removed the relationship should begin to repair.

If the pathology is false, changing the targeted parent's behavior will not change the child's rejection. The child will continue to maintain the barrier because the child's behavior is not actually under the stimulus control of the targeted-parent relationship. The behavior is being

### Breach and Repair: The Treatment Principle of Attachment

The core treatment principle in attachment is repair. Tronick and Gold describe repair as the central action of human interaction: mismatches occur, breaches occur, and healthy relationships repeatedly repair them. [Verify quotations from The Power of Discord from seminar slide.]

Normal and healthy parent-child relationships are not continuously synchronized. Much of ordinary interaction consists of mismatch followed by repair. The developmental value lies not in avoiding every breach but in learning that disconnection can be repaired, negative emotional states can change, and relationships can survive conflict.

This is why leaving an attachment breach unrepaired is so damaging. In a cooperative parentchild relationship, attachment treatment is often conceptually straightforward: identify the barrier, change the parenting that creates it, and support the repair sequence. The attachment system itself supplies the motivational energy for reconnection.

The difficulty in abusive or factitious pathology is that the necessary conditions for repair are obstructed. An abusive parent may remain dangerous, addicted, violent, or otherwise unavailable. In the induced pathology, the allied parent may actively prevent repair because the child's rejection serves the allied parent's psychological needs. The failure of repair is then not an accidental treatment problem; it is part of the pathology.

### Factitious Attachment and Anxiety Pathology

One form of false attachment pathology is conceptualized as Factitious Disorder Imposed on Another: a false attachment disorder is created in the child and presented as though it were caused by the targeted parent, while the symptoms serve secondary gains for the allied parent.

A related presentation can involve intense phobic anxiety toward the targeted parent, sometimes taking the form of panic-like symptoms associated with anticipated contact. The child may report severe anxiety merely thinking about being with the parent, may avoid the parent, and may endure contact with marked distress. When the targeted parent is objectively safe and there is no corresponding danger, the anxiety presentation must be examined as potentially factitious or induced rather than assumed to be evidence of danger.

The clinical reasoning again returns to the attachment system. A primary motivational system designed to maintain proximity to the parent is not ordinarily organized as a phobia of the parent. When such an apparently phobic presentation occurs in the absence of an abusive parent, the clinician must examine the interpersonal context in which the fear has been created, reinforced, and maintained.

### Domains of Competence Required for Diagnosis

Assessment of severe attachment pathology in the family courts is inherently multidisciplinary within professional psychology. Competent diagnosis requires knowledge of attachment, child abuse and complex trauma, delusional thought disorders, factitious disorders, personality pathology, and family systems.

Attachment knowledge is needed to identify the organization and direction of the child's attachment system. Child-abuse competence is needed to assess Path A and to recognize the protective obligations activated by the symptom. Competence in thought disorders is needed to assess a possible persecutory delusion in the allied parent and child. Knowledge of factitious disorders is needed to understand false symptom production for secondary gain. Family-systems knowledge is needed to identify cross-generational coalitions, enmeshment, and inverted hierarchies. Personality-pathology knowledge is needed to recognize splitting, absence of empathy, psychological control, and the narcissistic-borderline organization driving the transgenerational reenactment.

The narcissistic and borderline variants may differ somewhat in emphasis. The borderline presentation may involve greater anxiety and a larger number of abuse allegations, with more CPS involvement. The narcissistic presentation may carry a stronger element of anger, punishment, and weaponization of the child against the former spouse. Both variants can involve persecutory delusions and psychological spousal abuse of the targeted parent through the child's induced pathology.

### Duty to Protect and the Clinical Purpose of Diagnosis

Two abuse pathologies may therefore require simultaneous assessment: child psychological abuse and psychological spousal or partner abuse. The child may be harmed through the induction of severe attachment pathology, while the targeted parent may be harmed through the use of the child and the child's induced symptoms as a weapon within the former spousal conflict.

The clinical purpose of diagnosis is protection and treatment. When a child is rejecting a parent and the parents are making competing allegations about the source of the pathology, the mental health system should provide a timely risk assessment, an accurate diagnosis, and a treatment plan addressing the actual cause. The central question is not which parent 'deserves' custody. The clinical question is which parent is producing the child's pathology and what treatment and protective response are required.

A child rejecting a parent is therefore not a vague family-court phenomenon. It is an attachmentsystem symptom. The diagnosis begins with understanding the motivational architecture of attachment, identifying whether the child has an organized strategy for bonding, locating the barrier to attachment, and determining whether the pathology is authentic or induced. From there, the assessment proceeds into the relevant domains of abuse, trauma, delusion, personality, factitious pathology, and family systems.

Accurate diagnosis provides the path to effective treatment. If the targeted parent is abusive, protect the child and treat the abusive parenting and trauma. If the targeted parent is normal-
