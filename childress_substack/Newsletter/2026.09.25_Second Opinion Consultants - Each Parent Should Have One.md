Disputed Diagnoses are Resolved through 2nd & 3rd Opinion

In healthcare, the appellate system for a disputed diagnosis is second opinion. Any diagnosis returned into the family courts will be a disputed diagnosis. The legal system is an adversarial system, and one parent or the other will challenge any diagnosis returned into the legal system regarding the cause of the family conflict. 
So, let's get the second and even third opinion right at the start. 
The court should designate a primary treating therapist for the family conflict. Diagnosis guides treatment. It will be the obligation of the treating family therapist to render an accurate diagnosis regarding the cause of the family conflict. 

Path A Causality: that the targeted parent is somehow abusively maltreating the child, causing an authentic attachment pathology toward that parent,

Path B Causality: that the allied parent is psychologically abusing the child (DSM-5 V995.51) by creating an induced persecutory delusion (DSM-5 297.1) and false (factitious) attachment pathology in the child (DSM-5 300.19). 
That’s the differential diagnosis for a child rejecting a parent. It will be the obligation of the involved therapist who has duty of care for the child and family, to conduct a proper risk assessment for child abuse to the appropriate differential diagnoses for each parent and to develop an effective treatment plan to fix the problem.
Any diagnosis returned into the legal system, especially a child abuse diagnosis, will be disputed by one parent or the other. The appellate system in healthcare for a disputed diagnosis is second opinion. So the court should allow each parent-litigant to appoint a second opinion consultant of their choosing to participate through telehealth in the initial diagnostic assessment of the child and family. 
This will result in potentially three mental health reports being considered by the court for its custody related decision-making. The primary diagnostic report from the selected family therapist, along with two consulting reports.
Second & Third Opinion Consultation
A single family therapist should be selected to conduct the Clinical Diagnostic Assessment to the appropriate differential diagnoses for each parent. This selected family therapist will have duty of care for the child and family.
Each litigant parent is making abuse allegations against the other parent, either directly or implied. The court should allow each litigant parent to appoint a second opinion consultant of their choosing to participate in the clinical diagnostic assessment through telehealth, and to consult with the primary therapist who is conducting the Clinical Diagnostic Assessment.

From Improving Diagnosis in Healthcare: “Clinicians may refer to or consult with other clinicians (formally or informally) to seek additional expertise about a patient’s health problem. The consult may help to confirm or reject the working diagnosis or may provide information on potential treatment options. If a patient’s health problem is outside a clinician’s area of expertise, he or she can refer the patient to a clinician who holds more suitable expertise. Clinicians can also recommend that the patient seek a second opinion from another clinician to verify their impressions of an uncertain diagnosis or if they believe that this would be helpful to the patient.”
Improving Diagnosis in Healthcare (2015). National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine; Institute of Medicine; Board on Health Care Services; Committee on Diagnostic Error in Health Care; Erin P. Balogh, Bryan T. Miller, and John R. Ball, Editors
My Role as a Consultant to Parents & the Court
My role in the family courts is often as a second opinion engaged by the attorneys of one litigant parent to provide consultation and record review, typically of forensic custody evaluations and court-involved mental health treatment reports and clinical notes.
I recently submitted a declaration in a court matter, this is how I describe my involvement:

Qualifications and Background I am a Licensed Clinical Psychologist in the State of Washington (License No. 61538481). I hold a Doctorate in Clinical Psychology (Psy.D.) and have over 30 years of clinical experience specializing in child development, parent-child attachment pathology, developmental psychopathology, and complex family dynamics. My professional background includes tenure as Clinical Director for a multi-university assessment and treatment center for children in foster care. I actively consult in family law matters involving high-conflict custody disputes, parent-child attachment problems, and court-involved child and family pathology. 

Scope of Engagement and Ethical Boundaries (APA Standard 9.01b) I have been retained by the Petitioner’s attorney, <name>, as a clinical psychology consultant to provide expert document review, clinical reasoning analysis, and guidance regarding diagnostic and treatment frameworks. 

I have not conducted clinical interviews or formal examinations of the child <name>, the Petitioner <name>, or the Respondent <name>. 

Pursuant to American Psychological Association (APA) Ethics Code Standard 9.01(b) (Bases for Assessments), I do not render formal diagnostic determinations for any individual party in this matter, nor do I offer specific recommendations regarding residential schedules or custody dispositions. 

My statements in this declaration are offered strictly within the scope of a document review and expert clinical consultation (APA Ethics Code Standard 9.01(c), grounded in established scientific knowledge and practice standards (APA Ethics Code Standard 2.04) to assist the Court’s decision-making regarding diagnostic and treatment parameters for the child. 
I am engaged by a litigant-parent’s attorney as a consultant in clinical psychology - diagnosis and treatment. The other litigant parent can also hire their own consultant of their choosing. 
The three selected mental health professionals, the designated family therapist and the two consultants selected by the parents, can jointly collaborate on the Clinical Diagnostic Assessment necessary to render an accurate diagnosis for which parent is causing the child’s attachment symptoms - and what the treatment plan should be.
Child Abuse Allegations by Each Parent
Each parent is making abuse allegations against the other. 
The allied parent and the child’s symptoms are making direct or implied allegations that the parenting of the targeted parent is abusive towards the child and is creating the child’s authentic attachment pathology of rejecting the targeted parent. 
The targeted parent is making the counter-allegation that the allied parent is psychologically abusing the child by inducing a shared persecutory delusion and false (factitious) attachment pathology in the child. 
In all cases of court-involved custody conflict in which a child is rejecting a parent, a proper risk assessment for child abuse needs to be conducted to the appropriate differential diagnoses for each parent.
Differential Diagnostic Questions
Throughout healthcare, diagnosis guides treatment. The court’s decision-making will be much improved when it has an accurate diagnosis returned from the mental health system regarding which parent is abusing the child. 
In all cases of a child rejecting a parent, that symptom in itself, of a child rejecting a parent, implicates child abuse in a differential diagnostic pattern. 
In all cases of a child rejecting a parent, a proper risk assessment for child abuse needs to be conducted to the appropriate differential diagnoses for each parent. 
Any diagnosis returned into the legal system will be a disputed diagnosis. Each litigant-parent should be allowed to appoint a second opinion consultant of their choosing to participate in the Clinical Diagnostic Assessment through telehealth.

Dr. Childress:
Let’s talk about the Substack article on a Second Opinion Consultation model for the family courts. This is a standard clinical psychology healthcare solution to the family court adversarial system. Healthcare handles disputed or unclear diagnoses all the time, and the clinical psychologists are the profession with direct supervision. To become licensed, most psychologist standards require one year of supervised pre-doctoral training, and one year of supervised post-doctoral training. Putting other doctors in the room is entirely fine in the healthcare system of doctoring. 
What happened is that in the 1980s when professional psychology first began to encounter the pathology surrounding high-conflict custody litigation, the parallel process of staff-splitting surrounding the personality pathology of the parent caused the (unfamiliar) mental health professionals to be drawn into the “sides” created by the splitting pathology and narratives in the family. Each parent would have their dueling mental health testimony and opinions. This created professional conflict without ultimate clarity. 
It was into this problematic situation that the forensic psychologists entered from their adjacent court role of offering opinions to the court on competence issues. The forensic psychologists proposed to provide the court with “neutral” and unbiased mental health information related to the court’s custody decisions, and sought to avoid diagnostically “labeling” the parents as “narcissistic” and “borderline.” They brought a new paradigm to the mental health service delivery system - different than the clinical paradigm of diagnosis and treatment. 
From AFCC Guidelines: “I.4 Forensic Evaluation Parenting plan evaluations are forensic evaluations for use in developing court orders rather than clinical evaluations. Forensic evaluations involve the application of knowledge and skills from the mental health professions to the resolution of legal matters, whereas clinical evaluations aid in the diagnosis of psychological disorders for mental health treatment. In some jurisdictions, parenting plan evaluations may be mistakenly referred to as a “clinical” evaluation in orders of appointment. This is problematic because, unlike clinical evaluations, forensic evaluations are performed for the express purpose of assisting the parties and courts in reaching legal determinations that affect the rights and liberties of individuals.” 
From  Simon & Stahl 2020: “Because forensic psychology is truly a new field, many child custody evaluators, having been trained only in the clinical tradition, bring to their work what we call a clinical mindset rather than a forensic mindset. Despite what we see as a clear and convincing argument for using a forensically informed model when conducting child custody evaluations, there are still those who argue that a clinically informed approach to child custody evaluations is appropriate and preferable. We disagree with the clinically informed approach.” - 
Simon & Stahl (2020): Forensic Psychology Consultation in Child Custody Litigation: A Handbook for Work Product Review, Case Preparation, and Expert Testimony (2nd edition). American Bar Association.
A second opinion model is not available with the novel paradigm of forensic custody evaluations that take 6- to 9-months to complete for a cost of ~$20,000+ per assessment. Doubling the cost of a forensic custody evaluation into two evaluations is too expensive, and how is the second opinion selected? Plus the coordination of professional schedules may add further delays to an assessment procedure that already takes an excessively long time to complete. So the second opinion consultation model from healthcare was unavailable within the forensic custody evaluation paradigm. 
But the moment we re-apply the standard principles of healthcare, we can obtain a second and third opinion easily through telehealth for a six- to eight-week clinical diagnostic assessment, that can be returned for about $2,500 per psychologist. Allowing each parent litigant to hire their own consultant of their choosing, also solves selecting the second-opinion - select both, with each parent paying for their own consultant and sharing the cost of the primary family therapist who has duty of care. 
The earlier problem with the clinical psychology paradigm (diagnosis and treatment) was that it resulted in professional conflict and an absence of clarity. That’s a problem of application, not paradigm. Turn the post-assessment conflict into during-the-assessment collaboration, and give full voice to the professional issues for the court’s understanding and review - then the court makes its decisions based on its authority and consideration of all the evidence before it and the application of the law.

Yes. I think the strongest version of the Second Opinion Consultation model is to frame it not as a new invention, but as a return to ordinary healthcare architecture.
The historical problem you are describing is important because it separates failure of implementation from failure of the clinical paradigm itself. The earlier clinical system produced dueling experts because the professionals were brought in after the family system had already organized everyone into opposing camps. The clinicians then became downstream participants in the same split: one parent had one clinician, the other parent had another, and the conflict between professional opinions appeared to confirm that clinical psychology itself could not produce clarity.
The forensic response was structurally understandable: create a single “neutral” evaluator who would assist the court rather than treat the family. But that solved the problem by changing paradigms. The AFCC distinction you quoted is explicit: parenting-plan evaluations are forensic evaluations “for use in developing court orders rather than clinical evaluations,” while clinical evaluations are directed toward diagnosis for treatment. The Simon and Stahl passage makes the same paradigm distinction from the forensic side: they explicitly reject bringing a “clinical mindset” into custody evaluation.
Your proposal says, in effect: we don’t need to solve the shortcomings of clinical psychology by abandoning clinical psychology. We need to organize the clinical process correctly.
That gives the article a very coherent historical arc:
First: the pathology created professional splitting. Independent clinicians appeared on opposite sides, producing contradictory opinions after the fact.
Second: forensic psychology responded by centralizing authority in one neutral evaluator and shifting the purpose from diagnosis-and-treatment to assisting legal decision-making.
Third: that forensic architecture inadvertently made ordinary healthcare quality-control mechanisms difficult or impossible. A conventional second opinion becomes economically and operationally absurd if the “opinion” requires another six-to-nine-month, five-figure custody evaluation.
And then comes your solution:
Move the disagreement upstream.
Instead of waiting until one professional completes an assessment and then having another professional attack it, place the professionals together during the diagnostic process.
That is the conceptual advance.
The primary clinician has duty of care and conducts the actual Clinical Diagnostic Assessment. Parent A chooses a consultant. Parent B chooses a consultant. Each consultant is free to raise hypotheses, challenge evidentiary assumptions, request consideration of relevant information, question diagnostic reasoning, and identify areas requiring further assessment. They do not control the treating clinician. They do not make the court’s decision. They provide professional consultation.
Then the primary clinician says, essentially:
> Here is the diagnosis I reached, here is the evidence supporting it, here are the competing hypotheses considered, and here is why I reached this conclusion.
Consultant A can say:
> I agree, with these qualifications.
Consultant B can say:
> I disagree for these specific reasons.
Now the disagreement has become information rather than professional warfare.
That is much closer to what medicine does all the time. Doctors disagree. Consultation does not become a crisis merely because another doctor enters the room. In fact, difficult cases often become more collaborative as complexity increases.
And your cost/selection observation is elegant because the adversarial structure itself solves part of the logistics problem. There is no need for the court to locate one supposedly universally acceptable “neutral” second-opinion psychologist. Each parent chooses their own consultant. Each parent pays their own consultant. The parents divide the cost of the treating clinician. The court receives the work of all three.
So the architecture becomes:
Primary clinician
* duty of care
* diagnosis
* treatment plan
* direct assessment
Parent-selected consultant A
* second-opinion consultation
* professional challenge and input
* no duty-of-care substitution
Parent-selected consultant B
* same role for the other litigant
Court
* retains legal authority
* receives the primary clinical findings and any material areas of professional agreement or disagreement
* applies the law to the evidence
There is something else I think is important in what you are proposing. It changes what “bias” means.
The forensic solution tries to eliminate bias by finding a neutral evaluator.
Your solution accepts something more realistic: people have perspectives, hypotheses, information sources, and professional judgments. Rather than pretending those can be eliminated, the system makes them visible and contestable.
Each parent’s consultant is openly selected by that parent. Nobody has to pretend otherwise. Their reasoning can be examined. The primary clinician remains professionally independent. The court knows exactly where every professional sits in the architecture.
That may actually create more epistemic transparency than asking one evaluator to somehow occupy a perspectiveless position.
And this connects directly to the P-R Log work you have been developing. A consultant no longer needs to fly somewhere, conduct another complete assessment, or write a traditional 50-page rebuttal evaluation. With telehealth, digital records, structured document review, and AI-augmented reasoning, the consultant can participate efficiently throughout the diagnostic process.
So your ~$2,500 estimate is not just a lower price point. It reflects a different unit of professional work. The consultant isn’t replicating the assessment. The consultant is supplying expertise into the assessment.
That distinction belongs prominently in the article:
> A second opinion does not require a second complete evaluation.
That is how healthcare consultation normally works. A consulting specialist does not necessarily repeat every procedure already performed by the primary doctor. The consultant reviews the available findings, asks additional questions, examines the patient when appropriate, identifies missing information, and contributes specialized expertise.
There is also an important answer here to the historical objection, “But we already tried having each side bring experts, and it was a mess.”
Your answer is essentially:
Yes. Because we structured it as adversarial testimony rather than collaborative consultation.
The old arrangement was:
assessment → opinion → competing expert → professional conflict → court
Your model is:
assessment + concurrent consultation → transparent diagnostic reasoning → areas of agreement/disagreement → court
That’s a materially different system.
And I think that gives you a particularly strong closing concept for the Substack piece:
The problem was never that there were too many psychologists in the room. The problem was that we waited until after the assessment was finished to put them there.
Bring them in at the beginning. Let them talk to one another. Let them examine the same evidence. Let them challenge one another’s reasoning. Let the clinician with duty of care reach the diagnosis and treatment plan. And if disagreement remains, preserve it transparently for the court.
That is ordinary professional consultation transformed for the realities of an adversarial legal system.
