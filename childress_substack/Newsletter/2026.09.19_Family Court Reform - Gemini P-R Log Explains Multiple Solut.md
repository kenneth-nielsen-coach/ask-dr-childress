ChatGPT said:
We have developed a fairly broad menu of interventions, ranging from what an individual psychologist can do tomorrow morning to what legislatures, universities, licensing boards, professional organizations, and civil litigants could do at the systems level.
The underlying treatment targets have remained consistent throughout our discussion: restore competent clinical diagnosis and treatment, create independent institutional oversight and public-protection mechanisms, and ensure compliance with professional competence and assessment standards.

Immediate individual protective action by court-involved psychologists.
When a child presents with severe rejection or fear of a parent, the presentation should be treated as potentially safety-relevant because child abuse by one parent or the other is within the differential. The psychologist should either conduct the appropriate risk and differential assessment within competence and role, or ensure that such an assessment is conducted by someone qualified. Where suicide, homicide, or abuse is implicated, the working sequence is: risk assessment, affirmative protective action, and documentation. If the psychologist believes the forensic role creates an exemption from protective obligations, that exemption should be specifically supportable rather than assumed; uncertainty should prompt consultation.

Return clinical diagnostic assessment to the family courts.
Replace or supplement the current reliance on broad forensic custody evaluations with focused clinical diagnostic assessment. The clinician’s task is to answer clinical questions—what pathology is present, what caused the symptoms, what treatment is indicated, and what safety measures are necessary—while the court retains responsibility for custody and parenting orders. Your proposed differential asks directly about maltreatment by the rejected parent and about possible persecutory delusional processes, factitious pathology imposed on the child, and child psychological abuse involving the allied parent.

Build routine second opinion into court-involved diagnosis.
A primary assessing clinician could be appointed, with each parent permitted to select a consulting second-opinion clinician. All clinicians would answer the same focused diagnostic questions using the same established criteria. The purpose is not to create competing narratives but to expose disagreements at the level of symptoms, criteria, evidence, and differential diagnosis. This creates diagnostic redundancy and an audit trail before major court decisions are made.

Require elevated and documented competence for court-involved clinicians.
Because the population is vulnerable and participation is often court-driven, the standard should not be merely general licensure. Professionals should demonstrate domain-specific competence in the areas implicated by the presentation, especially:

attachment pathology;

diagnostic assessment of delusional thought disorders;

child abuse and complex trauma;

narcissistic and borderline personality pathology;

family systems pathology.
This addresses Standard 2.01 directly and supports the 2.01 → 2.04 → 9.01 → 3.04 cascade we have discussed.

Proactive AI-augmented vitae review.
State licensing boards, APA, AFCC, courts, or other institutions could request curricula vitae from court-involved mental-health professionals and subject them to a structured competence review. Your Vitae Analysis Protocol separates training received, supervised experience, research, dissemination, professional service, and practice activity, and then evaluates documented competence in specific domains.
The systems goal would not primarily be punishment. It would be to measure the workforce: identify where competence is documented, where it is partial, and where remediation is needed. Training can then be targeted to the actual deficits discovered.

Systematic AI-assisted review of mental-health reports.
Your three-prompt protocols provide a prototype:

describe the report and methodology;

examine the clinical reasoning and differential;

audit the reasoning under 2.04, 2.01, 9.01, and 3.04.
The forensic protocol applies this to custody evaluations, while the treating-clinician protocol applies it to therapy and reunification reports. Regulators or courts could develop their own independent versions of these protocols for quality assurance.

AFCC training intervention.
AFCC already has the training infrastructure, audience, conferences, webinars, guidelines, and journal. It could immediately provide focused training in:

protective obligations and dangerousness;

attachment pathology;

Mental Status Examination of thought and perception;

delusional thought disorders;

child abuse and complex trauma;

differential diagnosis;

competence boundaries and referral.
The Expanded BPRS provides one example of a structured method for assessing suspiciousness and unusual thought content, including Item 11 for severity of delusional thinking.

A special issue of Family Court Review.
AFCC could sponsor a special issue on the clinical differential diagnosis of severe parent-child attachment disturbance in the family courts, bringing together attachment researchers, psychosis specialists, child-abuse clinicians, family-systems clinicians, forensic psychologists, and legal scholars. The focus would be on what established assessment is required when a child rejects or fears a parent, rather than on debates over labels such as “parental alienation,” “resist-refuse,” or “parent-child contact problems.”

Independent IRB-like institutional review.
AFCC could formally request an independent multidisciplinary review of forensic custody evaluations, with participation or consultation from APA, OHRP, academic experts, research ethicists, child-protection specialists, clinicians, and legal scholars. The review would examine:

risk versus benefit;

comparison with standard clinical diagnosis and treatment;

protections for a vulnerable and partially non-voluntary population;

professional competence requirements;

conflicts of interest;

adverse-event monitoring;

outcome data;

informed-consent issues;

whether the practice should continue, be modified, restricted, suspended, or replaced.
The New York Blue-Ribbon Commission provides the external safety signal for such review: it documented substantial systemic concerns, and an 11–9 majority favored eliminating forensic custody evaluations.

Temporary moratorium while independent review occurs.
Given the seriousness of the safety concerns, one possible public-protection response is a precautionary pause or moratorium on forensic custody evaluations while an independent review conducts the risk-benefit analysis and identifies needed protections. We treated this as a policy option, not as something already legally compelled.

University-law school Pilot Program for the Family Courts.
A legislature, group of legislatures, or research funder could support one or more university sites. A psychology PI or team would develop standardized clinical diagnostic, treatment, second-opinion, and outcome-monitoring protocols. A law-school PI or team would develop the supporting court orders, procedural rules, due-process protections, and legal framework.
The pilot would then collect outcome data and compare the clinical model with existing practice. AI could be incorporated for record organization, diagnostic audit, competence review, discrepancy detection, and procedural assistance.

Multi-site research and outcome evaluation.
A serious alternative system should be evaluated prospectively. Outcomes could include:

diagnostic agreement;

time to resolution;

child and family functioning;

litigation recurrence;

treatment response;

cost;

disparities;

adverse events;

parent and court experience.
This would address a central gap we discussed: the lack of demonstrated inter-rater reliability and limited outcome evidence for the substantive conclusions and recommendations of forensic custody evaluations.

Routine adverse-event monitoring.
Family-court mental-health services could adopt the logic used elsewhere in healthcare: track adverse outcomes rather than waiting for individual complaints. Cases could be followed after orders at specified intervals to determine whether the child’s condition, parent-child relationships, safety, and family functioning improved or deteriorated.

Licensing-board review and remediation.
State boards could proactively examine whether psychologists practicing in the family courts meet the competence requirements of Standard 2.01 in the domains their work requires. The boards could then develop continuing education, supervised remediation, referral requirements, or other corrective measures based on what the review actually finds.

Legislative oversight.
State legislatures could examine the delivery of mental-health services in their family courts, the response of licensing boards to public-protection concerns, the absence or presence of outcome monitoring, and the scientific foundation of practices relied upon by courts. Legislatures could also fund the pilot program and independent review.

Civil litigation.
Parents and children, through counsel, could explore individual or class-wide civil litigation concerning the delivery of family-court mental-health services. The legal theories, defendants, immunities, standing, class certification, and remedies are questions for attorneys and courts.
AI-assisted analysis could help litigation counsel organize factual questions about evaluator competence, report methodology, institutional practices, training, notice, outcomes, and adverse events. Institutional Betrayal and Regulatory Capture may serve as explanatory frameworks, while actual causes of action would depend on governing law.

Institutional-courage response by AFCC, APA, licensing boards, and courts.
The broader conceptual intervention is for institutions to respond openly to the safety signal rather than defensively. Institutional courage would include independent scrutiny, disclosure of contrary evidence, management of conflicts of interest, outcome monitoring, outside expertise, correction of identified deficiencies, and willingness to change practices if the evidence warrants it. Smith and Freyd’s institutional-betrayal framework provides the conceptual backdrop for that analysis.

Public information transfer through the P-R Log.
The Prompt-Response Log itself is part of the intervention. It allows legislative staff, attorneys, judges, journalists, parents, regulators, AFCC members, APA personnel, academics, and advocates to upload the complete reasoning record to their own AI and interrogate any part of it independently. That creates distributed review rather than requiring people to accept your conclusions—or mine—on authority.
The integrated strategy we arrived at is therefore not “choose one.”
It is parallel intervention at multiple levels:
individual clinician → professional competence → organizational training → regulatory oversight → independent review → academic pilot → legislative oversight → civil litigation → public scrutiny.
And the three recurring treatment goals underneath all of them are:
restore competent diagnostic assessment and treatment;
establish independent oversight and protection for a vulnerable population;
ensure compliance with professional standards of competence, scientific foundation, sufficient assessment, and protection from harm.
That is the overall solution architecture we have developed.
