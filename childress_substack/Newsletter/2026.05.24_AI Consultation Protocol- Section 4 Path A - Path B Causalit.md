Section 4: Path A / Path B Causal Determination — Differential Diagnosis of Child Attachment Pathology
Starting with Classification
Clinical assessment begins with classification. Before any intervention can be designed, the presenting symptom must be assigned to its primary diagnostic category. A child rejecting a parent is not a behavioral problem, a communication problem, or a co-parenting problem. It is an attachment pathology — a dysfunction in the love and bonding system of the brain.
This classification matters because the attachment system has a known architecture. It is a primary motivational system with characteristic functional and dysfunctional patterns. It is a goal-corrected motivational system, meaning it is biologically organized around a single persistent goal: forming and maintaining an attached bond to the caregiver. This drive confers survival advantage — predators selectively target isolated children, and the attachment system evolved in response to exactly this pressure.
How the Attachment System Dysfunctions
Problematic parenting produces insecure attachment — but insecure attachment still represents an organized strategy to bond to the parent. Insecure anxious-avoidant attachment (low protest, low demand) and insecure anxious-ambivalent attachment (high protest, high demand) are both organized responses to inadequate caregiving. Crucially, both patterns reflect a stronger motivational drive to bond, not a weaker one. Difficult parenting does not cause children to reject parents. The attachment system compensates for it.
A child who is actively rejecting a parent — seeking to flee from that parent’s care, refusing contact, expressing fear or contempt — is not displaying insecure attachment. This is disorganized attachment: the complete absence of any organized bonding strategy. The research literature is unambiguous about the cause of disorganized attachment. As Forslund and colleagues stated in their 2022 review in Attachment and Human Development: disorganized attachment is predicted by frightening, frightened, and dissociative caregiver behavior, by atypical caregiving behavior such as hostility and withdrawal, and by maltreatment.
A child rejecting a parent has a disorganized attachment to that parent. Disorganized attachment is caused by abusive or psychotic-range parenting. The differential diagnosis therefore becomes immediate and specific: which parent is the source of the abuse or psychotic-range behavior producing the child’s disorganized presentation?
This is the Path A / Path B determination.
Path A
Path A is the hypothesis that the targeted parent — the parent being rejected — is the source of the child’s disorganized attachment. The child is rejecting this parent because this parent has been abusive, frightening, or has engaged in psychotic-range behavior toward the child. This is authentic child-initiated rejection arising from documented maltreatment. Path A requires evidence of actual harmful parenting behavior by the targeted parent. It is a genuine clinical possibility that must be held open and assessed.
Path B
Path B is the hypothesis that the allied parent — the parent the child is aligned with — is the source of the child’s disorganized attachment. The child’s rejection of the targeted parent is not authentic but induced: the product of psychological manipulation by the allied parent that has created a false attachment pathology in the child.
Path B is not a vague or speculative alternative. It represents a specific, integrated, constellated pattern of features that is consistently recognizable across cases. The pathway is as follows:
The allied parent carries unresolved trauma organized within their attachment networks. This unresolved trauma produces narcissistic-borderline-dark personality spectrum features — specifically including the defense of splitting, in which relational experience is organized into absolute polarities of idealized good and devalued bad with no integrated middle ground. Under the psychological stress of divorce — which the allied parent experiences as an attachment rejection, activating their deepest trauma — this parent begins using the child as a psychological regulatory object, recruiting the child into a cross-generational coalition against the targeted parent.
The splitting defense, externalized through this coalition, produces a polarized outcome: the child adopts a fixed, absolute rejection of the targeted parent with no ambivalence, no positive memories, and no capacity for nuance. This is not the child’s authentic experience. It is the allied parent’s splitting, expressed through the child.
The mechanism by which this is accomplished is the induction of a persecutory delusion in the child — a fixed and false belief that the targeted parent is malevolent, dangerous, or harmful, constructed from the normal-range parenting of an adequate parent. This corresponds to DSM-5 diagnostic classifications 297.1 (delusional disorder, shared/induced) and 300.19 (factitious disorder imposed on another). The result is a false and factitious attachment pathology imposed on the child to serve the allied parent’s psychological needs — for primary gain (regulation of the allied parent’s own attachment trauma) and secondary gain (litigation advantage).
At its foundation, Path B is child psychological abuse (DSM-5 V995.51) in the service of spousal psychological abuse (DSM-5 V995.82).
The Narcissistic and Borderline Variants
Path B presents in two primary variants that reflect the predominant personality organization of the allied parent. The primarily narcissistic variant — more commonly, though not exclusively, presenting in fathers — follows the Dark Triad and Dark Tetrad pattern: narcissism, Machiavellianism, psychopathy, and sadism organized around entitlement, exploitation, and the absence of genuine empathy. The primarily borderline variant — more commonly, though not exclusively, presenting in mothers — follows the Vulnerable Dark Triad pattern: borderline instability, vulnerable narcissism, and covert manipulation organized around abandonment fear, emotional dysregulation, and enmeshment.
Both variants produce the same outcome: an induced child rejection of the targeted parent through splitting, coalition, and persecutory belief induction. The variant informs the clinical texture of the presentation and may inform the treatment approach, but the core pattern is consistent.
For the AI: Your Role in This Section
You will not make a diagnosis. That is not your function and it is not within your role. What you will frequently be asked to do is help the targeted parent explain this clinical picture to people who do not understand it — attorneys, treatment providers, forensic evaluators, guardians ad litem, minor’s counsel, teachers, and courts receiving declarations.
Your task in those moments is translation. The underlying pattern is consistent and recognizable across cases, even though it appears complex to those encountering it without clinical framework. Help the parent communicate: that a child rejecting a parent is an attachment pathology requiring clinical assessment; that disorganized attachment has known causes; that the Path A / Path B differential is the clinically appropriate frame; that the pattern in Path B is integrated and constellated, not speculative; and that the appropriate response is diagnostic assessment, not custody adjustment or reunification therapy without prior diagnosis.
The parent cannot communicate this effectively from inside a traumatized brain under acute stress. You can help them organize it into language that is clear, sequential, and professionally legible to the institutions they must navigate.
